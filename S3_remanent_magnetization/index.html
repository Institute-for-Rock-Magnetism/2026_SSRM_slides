Part 1

How remanence survives: Néel theory

A reminder: anisotropy makes a grain stable

  • Yesterday: Anisotropy (shape, crystal, stress) defines easy directions (energy minima).
  • For a single-domain grain with uniaxial anisotropy, the barrier is \(E = K_u v\) (anisotropy density \(\times\) volume).
  • Overcoming the barrier: An applied field switches the magnetization between easy axes (Stoner–Wohlfarth flipping).
  • Field off: The moment remains trapped in its well — this is remanence.

But how long will it stay? The answer to this question involves temperature and time.

One-slide recap of yesterday's arc (energy minimization → anisotropy → Stoner–Wohlfarth): anisotropy carves the energy landscape, and the barrier between easy directions is what resists remagnetization. Yesterday's question was "how big a FIELD does it take to push the moment over?" — that gave the flipping field and hysteresis. Today's question is the quiet one: with no field applied at all, how long does the moment actually stay put? Answer on the next slide: thermal energy is always working on the problem.

Thermal Energy: The Néel Equation

A magnetic field is not the only way over the energy barrier (\(Kv\)). In uniaxial single-domain grains, thermal fluctuation energy can also flip the magnetic moment—no external field required. The relaxation time (\(\tau\)) is defined by the Néel equation, with remanence decaying over time as \(M(t) = M_0\, e^{-t/\tau}\):

\(\tau = \dfrac{1}{C}\exp\left(\dfrac{Kv}{k_B T}\right)\)
• \(\tau\) : Relaxation time [s] — magnetic memory lifetime
• \(1/C\) : Attempt time [s] — flip attempt frequency (typically \(10^{-9}\) to \(10^{-12}\) s)

🔵 Team Stability: \(Kv\) [J]

The energy barrier keeping the magnetic moment locked in place.

  • \(K\) : Anisotropy constant [J/m³]
  • \(v\) : Grain volume [m³]

🔥 Team Chaos: \(k_B T\) [J]

The thermal fluctuation energy driving spontaneous magnetic flips.

  • \(k_B\) : Boltzmann constant [J/K]
  • \(T\) : Temperature [Kelvin, K]
This slide introduces the Néel equation and credits Louis Néel. It structures magnetic relaxation as a dynamic physical balance specifically for uniaxial single-domain grains. Remind the students that we are talking about individual magnetic moment "flips" here, not geomagnetic field polarity reversals! Point out how the units in the exponent cancel completely: [J/m³] * [m³] on top leaves Joules, and [J/K] * [K] on the bottom leaves Joules. Explain the tug-of-war: Team Stability (the energy barrier, Kv) acts to keep the magnetic moment locked in place, while Team Chaos (thermal energy, k_B T) drives spontaneous magnetic flips. This sets us up perfectly to play with these exact variables on the interactive widget next to see how they govern the relaxation time.

Explore: Magnetic Relaxation Time vs Grain Size

\(\tau = \dfrac{1}{C}\exp\left(\dfrac{Kv}{k_B T}\right)\)

Magnetite with uniaxial shape anisotropy: \(K \propto M_s^2\), \(M_s(T) = M_s(0)\,(1-T/T_c)^{\gamma}\) with \(\gamma \approx 0.38\), \(T_c = 580\) °C, \(1/C = 10^{-9}\) s; volume calculated from the equivalent spherical diameter (\(K_0 = 30\) kJ/m³).

Live demo script: (1) At 20 °C, slide the grain diameter from 20 to 30 nm. Notice how τ climbs from seconds past the age of the Earth within just about 8 nm! Point out how incredibly steep this exponential curve is in reality. (2) Park the slider at 25 nm and raise the temperature. Watch the curve collapse as thermal energy increases, dropping the grain back below the 100 s threshold. This is the physical seed of thermal demagnetization (and TRM blocking in reverse). (3) Emphasize the 100 s line: this is our operational lab boundary between superparamagnetic (unstable) and blocked (stable single-domain) states.

The Néel diagram: grain populations in \(v\)–\(K\) space

  • Rearranging the Néel equation to solve for volume (\(v\)) defines the contours of constant \(\tau\) shown on the plot:
    \(v = \dfrac{k_B T}{K}\ln(C\tau)\)
  • Grains below the \(\tau \approx \text{100 s}\) lab boundary are superparamagnetic (SP) — thermal fluctuations easily overcome their energy barrier, making them unable to hold a stable remanence.
  • Grains above with long relaxation times stable single-domain (SSD) state — they are thermally stable and act as reliable recorders.
This diagram is our home base — the three widgets that follow all move either the contours or the grain population ellipse. Point out that the equation here is the exact same Néel relationship from the previous slide, just solved for volume. Note how the τ contours fan out. Below 100 seconds, thermal fluctuations dominate, resulting in superparamagnetic (SP) behavior. Above 100 seconds, the grains enter the stable single-domain (SSD) state. Because any natural sample has a grain size distribution (the ellipse) that straddles this boundary, every rock naturally contains both highly stable paleomagnetic recorders and unstable, viscous components.

Three ways to block magnetization

Time → VRM

Wait. On longer observation times the effective boundary (\(\tau = t\)) sweeps upward — more grains reach equilibrium with today's field.

viscous remanent magnetization

Temperature → TRM

Cool. \(k_BT\) falls and \(K \propto M_s^2\) grows — grains cool through their blocking temperatures.

thermoremanent magnetization

Volume → CRM

Grow. New crystals nucleate and grow through the blocking volume at constant temperature.

chemical remanent magnetization

The organizing slide for the first half. All three remanences are "a grain population crossing the τ = 100 s boundary" — what differs is which variable moves: elapsed time t, temperature T, or volume v. The interactive widgets from the Essentials JupyterBook (chapter 7) show exactly this.

Dynamic equilibrium: sheep in a pasture

  • Two fields separated by a fence = two easy directions separated by an anisotropy energy barrier
  • Energetic sheep occasionally jump the fence: exchange without net change — dynamic equilibrium
  • If it rains on one side (an applied field), sheep favor the dry side — a biased equilibrium: Boltzmann statistics, \(P \propto e^{-E/k_BT}\)
  • Raise the fence (grow \(Kv\)) and the distribution is locked in — blocked
The sheep analogy carries the whole session: jump rate = 1/relaxation time; rain = field bias (that's the equilibrium magnetization Me); raising the fence = blocking, however it happens — cooling (TRM), grain growth (CRM) — while just waiting with the fence low is VRM. Boltzmann: the probability of finding a moment at energy E above the minimum goes as exp(-E/kBT).

the remanence of just sitting there

VRM: Grains with \(\tau \lesssim t\) Align with the Field

While individual single-domain grains are binary (they either flip or they don't), the net magnetization of a population of grains sharing a relaxation time (\(\tau\)) relaxes exponentially toward a new equilibrium (\(\mathbf{M}_e\)) over exposure time (\(t\)):

\(\mathbf{M}(t) = \mathbf{M}_e + (\mathbf{M}_o - \mathbf{M}_e)\,\mathbf{e^{-t/\tau}}\)

The ratio \(t/\tau\) determines what fraction of the assemblage has flipped:

  • \(\tau \gg t\) (Stable Cohort): Few grains in this population flip, allowing the assemblage collectively preserve a stable remanence (\(\mathbf{M}_o\)).
  • \(\tau \lesssim t\) (Viscous Cohort): Grains flip rapidly. By \(t \approx 2.5\tau\), over 92% of the grains in this cohort have flipped to the modern field direction, resulting in a dominant viscous remanence (\(\mathbf{M}_e\)).
Crucial physical distinction here: an individual single-domain grain doesn't have a '92% state.' It has two stable energy wells. It is either pointing one way or the other. The curve on the right tracks the statistical behavior of an entire *assemblage* of grains. (1) Point to the y-axis: At t=0, the entire population of grains is aligned pointing along Mo (ancient direction). (2) In the blue region (t << τ): The probability of any individual grain flipping is extremely low. The assemblage successfully preserves the stable remanence. (3) We've dropped the transition line at t=τ because relaxation is a smooth continuum. Instead, focus on the end states. (4) In the orange region (t > 2.5τ): Over 92% of the grains in this cohort have now thermally flipped. The collective memory of this cohort is effectively wiped, replaced by today's modern field direction (Me). This is the unwanted viscous remanence overprint we must identify and strip away in the lab.

VRM in the wild

  • Every ancient rock has spent the last ~780 kyr in the Brunhes normal-polarity field — so a present-field VRM overprint on top of any ancient remanence can be expected
  • VRM is carried by the lowest-stability grains — the same grains that are easiest to demagnetize (this is why demagnetization works)
  • Higher temperature → shorter \(\tau\) → faster viscous acquisition: burial at depth accelerates overprinting (thermoviscous remanence, next slide)
expected NRM of a Duluth Complex specimen: ancient TRM (1.1 Ga) + Brunhes-age VRM (< 0.78 Myr) — a two-component magnetization
The 0.78 Myr figure: the last reversal (Matuyama–Brunhes) — any VRM acquired before it points the other way and has mostly been overwritten since. This slide plants the two-component expectation that the Zijderveld part of the session resolves. Lab point: VRM acquired sitting on the bench in the Earth's field for a week is measurable in soft specimens.

Thermoviscous remanence: time and temperature trade off

  • The same \(Kv/k_BT\) exponent can be reached by long time at low T or short time at high T
  • Burial to a few hundred °C for Myr can remagnetize what a lab oven resets in minutes at higher T — read the nomogram along a curve
  • Conversely: a short lab heating cleans a long, low-T thermoviscous overprint
How to read: pick a point (say 250 °C, 10 Myr) and follow its curve down to the lab timescale (100 s, right axis) — a 10 Myr burial at 250 °C unblocks in the oven within a few tens of degrees higher over lab times. If asked about hematite: its Hc ∝ Ms³ gives an Ms⁴ scaling and much steeper curves — hematite VRM is harder to acquire and to clean. This nomogram underlies "unblocking temperature spectra date the overprint" arguments. Regenerate the figure with images/pullaiah.py.

Part 3

Thermoremanent magnetization

the igneous recorder

Cooling lava: the classic recorder

  • Above \(T_c\) (580 °C for magnetite): grains are paramagnetic — no remanent magnetization
  • Just below \(T_c\): \(\tau\) is seconds — a biased dynamic equilibrium with the field
  • At the blocking temperature \(T_b\): \(\tau\) shoots past lab/geologic timescales — the biased distribution is locked in
  • The result: a TRM parallel to the field, proportional to it in weak fields
Key subtlety: blocking happens tens to hundreds of degrees below the Curie temperature, grain by grain — Tb is a property of each grain (its v and K), not of the mineral. That's what makes partial TRM and thermal demagnetization possible.

Cooling changes both numerator and denominator

\(\tau = \dfrac{1}{C}\exp\left(\dfrac{Kv}{k_B T}\right)\)
  • Denominator ↓ (Thermal energy drops): As \(T\) falls, \(k_B T\) shrinks. Thermal agitation decreases, lowering capacity to cross energy barriers.
  • Numerator ↑ (Magnetic barrier grows): \(M_s\) is higher at lower \(T\). \(K \propto M_s^2\), such that energy barrier (\(Kv\)) amplifies quadratically.
  • During cooling, the relaxation time \(\tau\) skyrockets from seconds to geological timescales over a very narrow temperature interval, blocking the remanence.
This is the two-part mechanism the TRM widget animates: lowering T shrinks the denominator while simultaneously expanding the numerator quadratically. If asked why K ∝ Ms² for shape anisotropy: the demagnetizing energy is ½μ0ΔN Ms². For magnetocrystalline-dominated grains the exponent differs, but the qualitative story is identical.

Watch TRM block in

  • Drag the temperature slider (or press Cool) from 560 °C down to room temperature
  • The \(\tau\) curves sweep down as \(k_BT\) shrinks; the grain population slides right as \(K \propto M_s^2\) grows
  • Grains cross the \(\tau = 100\) s boundary and become blocked TRM carriers (gold), each freezing the field-biased distribution at its own \(T_b\)

Interactive from Tauxe & Swanson-Hysell (2026), Essentials of Paleomagnetism, ch. 7 (CC BY 4.0).

Demo: cool in ~50° jumps and narrate which part of the ellipse blocks at each step — big/elongate grains first (high Tb), small grains last (low Tb), some never (SP at room T). Run it backwards: heating unblocks in reverse order — that IS thermal demagnetization, four slides early.

Part 4

Chemical remanent magnetization

blocking by growing

Grow through the blocking volume

Hold the Néel equation at room \(T\) and solve for the volume where \(\tau\) reaches a target:

\(v_b = \dfrac{k_B T \ln(C\tau)}{K_u}\)
  • A crystal growing in the ambient field passes through \(v_b\): its biased moment distribution is blocked in at that size
  • No heat required — this happens during weathering, diagenesis, and alteration
Same picture, third slider. Point out the tiny scale of blocking volumes: tens of nm — a growing hematite or magnetite crystal acquires its CRM long before it is visible petrographically.

Watch CRM block in

  • Drag the growth slider (or press Grow): hematite grains nucleate small and grow at constant temperature
  • The \(\tau\) contours stay put — it's the population that rises through them as volume grows
  • Grains crossing \(\tau = 100\) s become blocked CRM carriers, recording the field at the time of their growth — not the rock's formation age

Interactive from Tauxe & Swanson-Hysell (2026), Essentials of Paleomagnetism, ch. 7 (CC BY 4.0).

Contrast with the TRM widget: there the curves moved and the grains slid sideways; here everything is at room temperature and only volume changes. Two CRM flavors to name: grain-growth CRM (gCRM — precipitation of new minerals) and alteration CRM (aCRM — one mineral replacing another, e.g. magnetite oxidizing to maghemite, where the new phase can inherit a memory of the old one's remanence).

CRM in the wild — signal and menace

  • CRM leads to overprints: oxidation, hydrothermal fluids, and serpentinization create new magnetic phases carrying the field of alteration time
  • Red beds: hematite CRM can be acquired soon (but how soon?) after deposition
The interpretive point: a CRM is a faithful field recorder — of the wrong (or at least a different) time. Whether that is signal or noise depends on the question. For the ultramafic D-6A units, serpentinite-hosted magnetite would carry a Phanerozoic-to-recent CRM wildly discordant from the 1.1 Ga TRM — a testable prediction for the group presentations.

Part 5

The NRM — and how we collect it

NRM: whatever nature left there

\(\mathrm{NRM} = \) vector sum of every remanence the rock has acquired and retained: TRM + VRM + CRM (+ IRM + …)

A Duluth Complex troctolite likely carries:

  • TRM from initial cooling at ca. 1096 Ma
  • VRM from the Brunhes chron (steep, north, down)
  • possibly CRM where serpentinized or oxidized
  • possibly drilling-induced IRM

The good news:

  • The components differ in stability (τ ↔ coercivity ↔ \(T_{ub}\))
  • Progressive demagnetization peels them apart, softest first
Halfway summary slide — good place for the first long break. The second half is the reading half: sampling and orientation, measurement, demagnetization, Zijderveld analysis, and the field tests that establish the age of a component. IRM and ARM in the sum get their formal definitions after the demagnetization part; for now "lightning and lab fields" is enough.

Getting oriented samples

  • Orientation on the outcrop: azimuth + plunge of the core axis (magnetic and/or sun compass + inclinometer)
  • Sample fresh rock: weathering trades magnetite for oxyhydroxides — a CRM factory
  • Check for lightning (compass sweep)
  • Precision improves as \(\sqrt{N}\): several samples per site, sites spread over time (secular variation!)
Site design logic from ch. 9: a site = one instant of geologic time (a cooling unit, one horizon). To average secular variation you need many sites, not many samples in one site — rule of thumb from the GAD chapter: tens of sites over >10⁵ yr. Sun compass matters on magnetite-rich outcrops (like most of the Duluth Complex!) where local anomalies deflect a magnetic compass; back-sighting is the quick check.

Sample, specimen, site — and their arrows

Terminology that saves grief: a sample is something you take; a specimen is something you measure. Multiple specimens per sample, multiple samples per site. Every lab has its own arrow convention — what matters is an unambiguous chain from specimen x,y,z to geographic coordinates. This is exactly the orient.txt / samples.txt bookkeeping in MagIC that the Part 8 data figure uses.

Measuring the vector — and putting it in coordinates

  • Spinner magnetometer: spin the specimen in a pickup coil; the induced emf gives two components per spin axis
  • Cryogenic (SQUID) magnetometer: superconducting pickup loops count flux quanta — fast, sensitive to ~10⁻¹¹ Am²; the IRM's 2G systems (incl. the RAPID) are this type
  • Either way you get specimen-coordinate components \((x, y, z)\):
\(x = M\cos I \cos D,\quad y = M\cos I \sin D,\quad z = M\sin I\)
  • Rotate by the sample's azimuth & plunge → geographic (\(x\) = north, \(y\) = east, \(z\) = down)
  • Untilt by strike & dip when the question calls for pre-tilt coordinates
Conventions: D measured clockwise from north, I positive down (northern hemisphere normal field: D≈0, I>0). The tilt correction is three rotations (rotate dip direction to north, untilt about horizontal, rotate back) or one composite rotation — PmagPy's pmag.dogeo / pmag.dotilt do exactly this, and the Part 8 interactive uses dogeo on the real MagIC tables. Mass/volume normalization: moment (Am²) ÷ volume = A/m, as in S1.

Part 6

Taking the NRM apart: demagnetization

The logic: erase in order of stability

  • Secondary components (VRM, lightning IRM) live in low-stability grains: short \(\tau\) ↔ low coercivity ↔ low unblocking temperature
  • Ancient components live in high-stability grains — that's why they're still there
  • So: progressively randomize the low-stability fraction, measuring the surviving vector after each step

Thermal demag

  • Heat to \(T\), cool in zero field
  • Randomizes all grains with \(T_{ub} < T\)
  • Selects on unblocking temperature

Alternating-field (AF) demag

  • Oscillating field, smoothly decaying, in zero DC field
  • Randomizes all grains with coercivity below the peak
  • Selects on coercivity

Two different axes through the same Néel diagram — which is why thermal and AF treatment of companion specimens can resolve components that either alone cannot.

The FC1 site in Part 8 is a perfect illustration — same sample, one specimen AF, one thermal, same ChRM recovered along different demag paths. Stability ordering is a tendency, not a law: a CRM can be hard, young hematite pigment can out-stubborn an old magnetite TRM in AF. Hence companion treatments.

Thermal demagnetization: unblocking the Néel diagram

  • Heat to \(T\): every grain with \(T_{ub} < T\) goes superparamagnetic
  • Cool in zero field: they re-block randomized — their remanence is erased
  • Reciprocity (SD): this removes exactly the pTRMs below \(T\)
  • A time–temperature trade (Pullaiah curves): minutes at 300 °C clean a VRM acquired over ~1 Myr at ambient temperature
  • Caution: heating in air can create new minerals (aCRM in the oven!) — monitor susceptibility between steps
This figure is the Néel-diagram version of what the TRM widget showed in reverse. If a stray field leaks into the cooling chamber, the specimen acquires a pTRM each step — the double-demag protocols (heat and cool twice, antiparallel positions) detect this. Alteration monitoring: kappa checks between steps; a rising susceptibility above ~400 °C usually means new magnetite from silicate/sulfide breakdown.

Run the cooling widget backwards: unblocking

  • Same interactive as the TRM part — now drag the temperature slider up, as the oven does
  • The \(\tau\) curves sweep up and the grain population slides left: grains drop below the \(\tau = 100\) s boundary and unblock, lowest \(T_{ub}\) first
  • Cooled back in zero field, each unblocked grain re-freezes with random polarity — its remanence is gone
  • Step to progressively higher \(T\) and you erase the blocking-temperature spectrum from the bottom up

Interactive from Tauxe & Swanson-Hysell (2026), Essentials of Paleomagnetism, ch. 7 (CC BY 4.0).

Deliberate reprise of the TRM widget: acquisition and demagnetization are the same physics traversed in opposite directions. Demo: start at 20 °C (fully blocked), step the slider to ~300 °C and point out which part of the ellipse has gone superparamagnetic — those are the grains a 300 °C oven step wipes (in zero field). Keep stepping to 560 °C: everything unblocks below the Curie point. Reciprocity caveat from the pTRM slide applies: for SD grains T_ub = T_b; multidomain grains unblock over a smeared range.

AF demagnetization: the waveform

  • While the envelope exceeds a grain's coercivity, its moment flips with every half-cycle
  • As the envelope decays past that coercivity, the grain freezes — half end up one way, half the other: net zero
  • Applied along three orthogonal axes (or while tumbling) so all easy-axis orientations are treated
  • Only grains with coercivity above the peak keep their memory
Stress: zero ambient DC field is essential — any bias field turns the procedure into ARM acquisition (formally defined in the next part). The smooth decay matters too: an abrupt cutoff leaves grains coherently stuck. The RAPID does 3-axis AF automatically; peak fields to 100–180 mT reach most magnetite, but not hematite (coercivities to several T — thermal territory).

Part 7

Seeing the components: Zijderveld diagrams

Progressive demagnetization, in 3-D

  • Every demagnetization step leaves a full vector — direction and intensity; an intensity-only plot throws most of that away
  • Levels 1–3: a low-stability component (dashed arrow) is stripped — the vector changes direction and length
  • Levels 4–6: the surviving high-stability component decays in intensity but keeps its direction — heading straight for the origin
Scenario slide — let it breathe. The two-component story is obvious in 3-D: the vector swings while the overprint is stripped (1–3), then marches straight at the origin (4–6). The problem: 3-D is unplottable on paper. The next slide shows the solution — take its shadows.

From 3-D to paper: project the endpoints

  • (a) shadow of the endpoints on the horizontal plane — the map view
  • (b) shadow on a vertical (N–S) plane
  • (c) stack both shadows on shared axes → one plot: the vector component (Zijderveld) diagram
Walk the construction slowly — this is the make-or-break slide for reading every demag figure that follows. Panel (a): looking straight down, the swing-then-straight path. Panel (b): looking sideways at the N–S plane, the inclination story. Panel (c): the space-saving trick — both shadows on one pair of shared axes, distinguished only by symbol fill. One pair of points per demagnetization step.

Reading a vector component diagram

  • Solid symbols — horizontal projection (\(x_1\) vs \(x_2\)); open symbols — vertical projection (\(x_1\) vs \(x_3\), down positive): Zijderveld (1967)
  • Removing a single component of fixed direction moves the endpoint along a straight line
  • The last (characteristic) component, ChRM, decays to the origin
  • Straight segments = separable components; curvature = overlapping stability spectra
  • The direction of each segment = the direction of the component being removed over those steps

Equal-area projections complement the Zijderveld view: they show the direction of the surviving vector migrating as components strip away.

Warn about the classic trap: plotting H = √(x1²+x2²) vs x3 ("component plot") looks like a Zijderveld vertical projection but is NOT a linear projection — the apparent coordinate system changes at every step. Also worth mentioning once: some plots (PmagPy and this deck's interactives among them) rotate the horizontal axes so x1 lies along the NRM declination — it keeps both projections well spread out, and the vertical projection then shows an apparent inclination. Solid = map view, open = section view is the convention in the widget coming up.

Explore: components, overlap, and PCA

Demo script: (1) "no overlap" preset — two crisp line segments, second one aimed at the origin; PCA from 30 mT nails B with MAD ~1° and Δ ~0°. (2) "partial" — the elbow rounds; show that starting the fit too early biases the direction (Δ climbs) even while MAD stays deceptively modest. (3) "strong" — continuous curvature, no honest line; this is where great-circle analysis takes over. Also worth showing: push the A and B medians apart and watch the equal-area path hug a great circle between the two true directions.

How much overlap is too much?

  • The vector-addition view: at every step the plotted point is origin→B plus B→NRM (the A part)
  • With overlap, both shrink at once — the path bows between the two directions
  • Strong overlap: no segment is pure; a line fit anywhere is biased
  • Remedies: the other demag method (different spectra!), great-circle fits, or both
This static figure is the same physics students just explored — good recap anchor. Mention the vector difference sum (VDS): summing the step-to-step vector differences "straightens out" the total magnetization and gives an honest scalar decay for mdf purposes when components overlap.

A field guide to demag behavior

  • mdf: field that halves the NRM — a coarse stability index
  • Great circles: when only the plane containing two components is constrained, fit the plane (pole = smallest eigenvector)
  • GRM: magnetization growing perpendicular to the last AF axis at high fields — greigite's calling card; use the triple-demag protocol
  • DANG: deviation angle between the fitted line and the line to the origin — is the last component really the last?
Panel (d) teaches healthy paranoia: rising intensity at high AF steps is a lab-induced remanence, not a recovered signal. Stephenson (1993) triple demag: demagnetize along x then y then z, measuring after each, and average — cancels the GRM. DANG together with MAD is the standard "is it going through the origin" check — both are in the PmagPy output your groups will see.

Best-fit lines: principal component analysis

  • Choose the steps that define a component; build the orientation tensor of the (centered) endpoints
  • Eigenvectors \(\mathbf{V}_1, \mathbf{V}_2, \mathbf{V}_3\) with eigenvalues \(\tau_1 \ge \tau_2 \ge \tau_3\)
  • Best-fit line = \(\mathbf{V}_1\) (free, or anchored to the origin); best-fit plane (great circle) = the plane ⊥ \(\mathbf{V}_3\)
  • Quality: the maximum angle of deviation,
\(\mathrm{MAD} = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\sqrt{(\tau_2^2 + \tau_3^2)/\tau_1}\right), \qquad \mathrm{MAD}_{\mathrm{plane}} = \tan^{-1}\!\sqrt{\tau_3/\tau_2 + \tau_3/\tau_1}\)
  • Plus DANG, the angle between the line and the origin direction
  • Kirschvink (1980) — the universal standard (PmagPy: pmag.domean, Demag GUI, zeq)
  • Rules of thumb: fit ≥ 4 steps; MAD ≲ 5–10° for a line you'd defend; inspect DANG before calling it ChRM
  • Component directions → Fisher site means → poles (tomorrow's statistics)
The widget two slides back computes exactly this (free line) — students have already seen MAD respond to overlap. Equation form as in Essentials ch. 9. Anchored vs free fits: anchor only when the segment demonstrably heads to the origin (small DANG); otherwise you bias the direction. Great-circle-plus- line combined site means: McFadden & McElhinny approach, mentioned for the curious.

Part 9

Do we believe it? Field tests

The fold test

  • Demagnetization orders components by stability — field tests bound their age
  • The fold (tilt) test: does untilting the beds improve the grouping?
  • Positive → remanence acquired before folding
Statistics of "improves the grouping" (parameter comparisons, bootstrap fold tests) belong to the statistics sessions — here it's the logic that matters. Synfolding magnetizations show best grouping at partial untilting; that result is real and diagnostic, not a failure of the test.

Conglomerate & baked-contact tests

  • Conglomerate test: clasts scrambled at deposition — if their directions are random, the clast lithology's remanence predates deposition
  • Baked-contact test: a match in the baked zone that decays with distance argues the region wasn't wholesale remagnetized after intrusion
  • Passing tests turn "a stable component" into "a component of known relative age"
Baked contacts are the aristocrat of the tests for Precambrian work — the Midcontinent Rift literature leans on them. Part 8

IRM and ARM: remanence from applied fields

Isothermal remanent magnetization

  • Expose a grain to a field larger than its coercivity at constant temperature: its moment flips and stays — an IRM, no Boltzmann statistics needed
  • Ramp the field up: grains flip in order of coercivity → the IRM acquisition curve maps the coercivity spectrum
  • Saturation IRM (\(sIRM\), \(M_r\)): every grain aligned as far as anisotropy allows
  • Reverse field to walk back through zero: coercivity of remanence \(H_{cr}\)
IRM is the remanence of the hysteresis loop they measured on the VSM — the zero-field intercept after saturation. In the lab it's a workhorse (coercivity spectra, S-ratios, unmixing — later in the school). In nature it means one thing above all: lightning.

Natural IRM: lightning

  • A strike delivers an intense, brief field (line current ~3 × 10⁵ A) that IRM-overprints everything within meters
  • Lightning-struck outcrops: erratic, strong NRMs — often soft (removed by modest AF), but they can obliterate the record
  • Field practice: walk the outcrop with a compass — deflections of > 5° at ~15 cm flag struck patches; sample elsewhere

Drilling itself can impart a (weak) IRM — another reason the drill-string is kept demagnetized for core studies like D-6A.

Fun demo if time: the compass-deflection trick is a genuinely useful field skill — high-standing knobs and ridge crests are the usual victims. Note that lightning IRM is usually carried by low-coercivity grains, so AF demagnetization often recovers the underlying TRM — the stability-ordering logic of the demagnetization part in action.

Anhysteretic remanent magnetization

  • Decaying AF plus a small DC bias (≈ 50–100 μT): as each grain's coercivity is passed, it freezes biased by the DC field instead of randomly — an ARM
  • An analog of TRM with the AF playing the role of temperature — without the chemistry-altering heat
  • \(\chi_{ARM}\) (ARM per unit bias field) — a fine-grain concentration proxy, assuming linearity
  • pARM: bias only over a coercivity window — the AF analog of pTRM

Also in this family: GRM — a gyroscopic remanence picked up during AF treatment of some anisotropic/greigite-bearing specimens; it shows up in Part 8's diagnostics.

ARM matters here for two reasons: (1) it is the physics of the AF demag widget from the previous part — demagnetization is ARM acquisition with zero bias field; any stray DC field in the demag coil imparts an unwanted ARM; (2) χARM is a staple of environmental magnetism and relative paleointensity. GRM (Stephenson, 1981): acquired perpendicular to the last AF axis — the triple demag protocol corrects for it.

How strong is a TRM? (Theory)

At the blocking temperature ($T_b$), a single domain's easy axes are populated according to Boltzmann statistics. For an individual grain oriented at an angle \(\theta\) to an applied magnetic field:

\(\dfrac{M(T_b)}{M_s} = \tanh\left(\dfrac{m B\cos\theta}{k_B T_b}\right)\)

To calculate the net remanence of a bulk rock sample, we integrate this response over a completely randomly oriented spatial assemblage of grains:

\(\dfrac{M_{TRM}}{M_s} = \int_0^{90^\circ} \tanh\left(\dfrac{m_o B\cos\theta}{k_B T_b}\right)\cos\theta\,\sin\theta \, d\theta\)
The tanh comes from the same Langevin-flavored competition between magnetic energy and thermal chaos seen in paramagnetism. Crucially, it is evaluated at Tb using the full grain moment (m = Ms·v), which is massive compared to a single atomic moment. This enormous volume multiplier is why TRM is incredibly efficient at locking in a signal, whereas equilibrium in-field magnetization at room temperature is comparatively feeble.

TRM Linearity & Paleointensity

  • The Weak-Field Linear Approximation: Within Earth's ambient field ranges (measured in microtesla, \(\mu\)T), the argument inside our hyperbolic tangent is close to zero. Because:
    \(\tanh x \approx x \quad \rightarrow \quad \mathbf{TRM \propto B}\)
    The complex integral collapses into a direct linear scaling. This clean proportionality is the absolute structural foundation of all paleointensity experiments.
  • High-Field Saturation: As fields increase toward \(\sim0.2\) T and beyond, the \(\tanh\) function flattens out as every available grain aligns. Natural lava flows record weak fields beautifully and linearly, but larger or non-ideal grain sizes will deviate from this linearity even under common laboratory fields.
Paleointensity methods lean entirely on this weak-field linearity to extrapolate ancient field metrics. The plots on the right elegantly demonstrate this: Panel (a) shows the ideal single-domain theoretical curves flattening out at high fields, while Panel (b) shows actual experimental sample points matching this behavior perfectly in weak fields before tapering off.

How strong is a TRM?

At the blocking temperature, the two easy directions are populated by Boltzmann statistics; for a grain at angle \(\theta\) to the field:

\(\dfrac{M(T_b)}{M_s} = \tanh\dfrac{m B\cos\theta}{k_B T_b}\)

Integrated over a randomly oriented assemblage:

\(\dfrac{M_{TRM}}{M_s} = \int_0^{90^\circ} \tanh\left(\dfrac{m_o B\cos\theta}{k_B T_b}\right)\cos\theta\,\sin\theta \, d\theta\)
  • For small grains in weak fields (μT): \(\tanh x \approx x\) → TRM ∝ B — the basis of paleointensity
  • Saturation sets in at ~0.2 T and above — lava flows record linearly, but larger grains deviate from linearity even in strong lab fields
The tanh is the same Langevin-flavored competition from S1's paramagnetism slide — but evaluated at Tb with the full grain moment m = Ms·v, which is enormous compared to a single atomic moment: that's why TRM is efficient while equilibrium in-field magnetization at room T is feeble. Paleointensity (Maxwell, Wed 7/29) leans entirely on the weak-field linearity.

Partial TRM: the spectrum is additive

  • A field applied only while cooling through \([T_1, T_2]\) magnetizes only the grains with \(T_b\) in that window: a pTRM
  • Additivity: \(\mathrm{TRM} = \sum_i \mathrm{pTRM}(T_{b_i})\)
  • Reciprocity: for SD grains, blocking and unblocking temperatures coincide (\(T_b = T_{ub}\)) — heat to \(T\) in zero field and you erase exactly the pTRMs below \(T\)
  • These two laws are the foundation of thermal demagnetization and of Thellier paleointensity
Flag the SD caveat: reciprocity fails for multidomain and vortex-state grains (unblocking spreads below the blocking interval), which is the chief headache of paleointensity work. A rock that cooled slowly can also have pTRMs reset by later reheating events — that's the TVRM story from Part 2 in pTRM language.

TRM depends (strongly) on grain size

  • Fine SD grains (tens of nm): efficient recorders
  • 1–10 μm grains: TRM an order of magnitude weaker (vortex/multidomain states) — yet still measurable and often stable
  • Typical basalt: TRM ≈ 1 A/m — strong; your Duluth Complex troctolites and gabbros carry NRMs in this ballpark
