Part 1

How remanence survives: Néel theory

A reminder: anisotropy makes a grain stable

  • Yesterday: Anisotropy (shape, crystal, stress) defines easy directions (energy minima).
  • For a single-domain grain with uniaxial anisotropy, the barrier is \(E = K_u v\) (anisotropy density \(\times\) volume).
  • Overcoming the barrier: An applied field switches the magnetization between easy axes (Stoner–Wohlfarth flipping).
  • Field off: The moment remains trapped in its well — this is remanence.

But how long will it stay? The answer to this question involves temperature and time.

One-slide recap of yesterday's arc (energy minimization → anisotropy → Stoner–Wohlfarth): anisotropy carves the energy landscape, and the barrier between easy directions is what resists remagnetization. Yesterday's question was "how big a FIELD does it take to push the moment over?" — that gave the flipping field and hysteresis. Today's question is the quiet one: with no field applied at all, how long does the moment actually stay put? Answer on the next slide: thermal energy is always working on the problem.

Thermal Energy: The Néel Equation

A magnetic field is not the only way over the energy barrier (\(Kv\)). In uniaxial single-domain grains, thermal fluctuation energy can also flip the magnetic moment—no external field required. The relaxation time (\(\tau\)) is defined by the Néel equation, with remanence decaying over time as \(M(t) = M_0\, e^{-t/\tau}\):

\(\tau = \dfrac{1}{C}\exp\left(\dfrac{Kv}{k_B T}\right)\)
• \(\tau\) : Relaxation time [s] — magnetic memory lifetime
• \(1/C\) : Attempt time [s] — flip attempt frequency (typically \(10^{-9}\) to \(10^{-12}\) s)

🔵 Team Stability: \(Kv\) [J]

The energy barrier keeping the magnetic moment locked in place.

  • \(K\) : Anisotropy constant [J/m³]
  • \(v\) : Grain volume [m³]

🔥 Team Chaos: \(k_B T\) [J]

The thermal fluctuation energy driving spontaneous magnetic flips.

  • \(k_B\) : Boltzmann constant [J/K]
  • \(T\) : Temperature [Kelvin, K]
This slide introduces the Néel equation and credits Louis Néel. It structures magnetic relaxation as a dynamic physical balance specifically for uniaxial single-domain grains. Remind the students that we are talking about individual magnetic moment "flips" here, not geomagnetic field polarity reversals! Point out how the units in the exponent cancel completely: [J/m³] * [m³] on top leaves Joules, and [J/K] * [K] on the bottom leaves Joules. Explain the tug-of-war: Team Stability (the energy barrier, Kv) acts to keep the magnetic moment locked in place, while Team Chaos (thermal energy, k_B T) drives spontaneous magnetic flips. This sets us up perfectly to play with these exact variables on the interactive widget next to see how they govern the relaxation time.

Magnetite with uniaxial shape anisotropy: \(K \propto M_s^2\), \(M_s(T) = M_s(0)\,(1-T/T_c)^{\gamma}\) with \(\gamma \approx 0.38\), \(T_c = 580\) °C, \(1/C = 10^{-9}\) s; volume calculated from the equivalent spherical diameter (\(K_0 = 30\) kJ/m³).

Point out how incredibly steep this exponential curve is in reality. (2) Park the slider at 25 nm and raise the temperature. (3) Emphasize the 100 s line: this is our operational lab boundary between superparamagnetic (unstable) and blocked (stable single-domain) states.

The Néel diagram: grain populations in \(v\)–\(K\) space

  • Rearranging the Néel equation to solve for volume (\(v\)) defines the contours of constant \(\tau\) shown on the plot:
    \(v = \dfrac{k_B T}{K}\ln(C\tau)\)
  • Grains below \(\tau \approx \text{100 s}\) are superparamagnetic (SP) — thermal fluctuations easily overcome their energy barrier, making them unable to hold a stable remanence.
  • Grains above with long relaxation times stable single-domain (SSD) state — they are thermally stable and act as reliable recorders.
This diagram is our home base — the three widgets that follow all move either the contours or the grain population ellipse. Point out that the equation here is the exact same Néel relationship from the previous slide, just solved for volume. Note how the τ contours fan out. Below 100 seconds, thermal fluctuations dominate, resulting in superparamagnetic (SP) behavior. Above 100 seconds, the grains enter the stable single-domain (SSD) state. Because any natural sample has a grain size distribution (the ellipse) that straddles this boundary, every rock naturally contains both highly stable paleomagnetic recorders and unstable, viscous components.

Three ways to block (lock in) magnetization

Time → VRM

Wait. As time progresses, the effective boundary (\(\tau = t\)) sweeps upward — more grains reach equilibrium with the field they are experiencing.

viscous remanent magnetization

Temperature → TRM

Cool. Thermal energy (\(k_BT\)) falls and anisotropy energy (\(K \propto M_s^2\)) grows — grains cool through their blocking temperatures.

thermoremanent magnetization

Volume → CRM

Grow. New crystals nucleate and grow through their blocking volume at constant temperature. \(Kv\) and \(\tau\) increase.

chemical remanent magnetization

The organizing slide for the first half. All three remanences are "a grain population crossing the τ = 100 s boundary" — what differs is which variable moves: elapsed time t, temperature T, or volume v. The interactive widgets from the Essentials JupyterBook (chapter 7) show exactly this.

Dynamic equilibrium: sheep in a pasture

  • Two fields separated by a fence = two easy directions separated by an anisotropy energy barrier
  • Energetic sheep occasionally jump the fence: exchange without net change — dynamic equilibrium
  • If it rains on one side (an applied field), sheep favor the dry side — a biased equilibrium: Boltzmann statistics, \(P \propto e^{-E/k_BT}\)
  • Raise the fence (grow \(Kv\)) and the distribution is locked in — blocked
The sheep analogy carries the whole session: jump rate = 1/relaxation time; rain = field bias (that's the equilibrium magnetization Me); raising the fence = blocking, however it happens — cooling (TRM), grain growth (CRM) — while just waiting with the fence low is VRM. Boltzmann: the probability of finding a moment at energy E above the minimum goes as exp(-E/kBT).

the remanence of just sitting there

VRM: Grains with \(\tau \lesssim t\) Align with the Field

While individual single-domain grains are binary (they either flip or don't), the net magnetization of a population of grains sharing a relaxation time (\(\tau\)) relaxes exponentially toward a new equilibrium (\(\mathbf{M}_e\)) over time (\(t\)):

\(\mathbf{M}(t) = \mathbf{M}_e + (\mathbf{M}_o - \mathbf{M}_e)\,\mathbf{e^{-t/\tau}}\)
  • \(\tau \gg t\) (Stable Cohort): Few grains in this population flip, allowing the assemblage collectively preserve a stable remanence (\(\mathbf{M}_o\)).
  • \(\tau \lesssim t\) (Viscous Cohort): Grains flip rapidly. By \(t \approx 2.5\tau\), over 92% of the grains in this cohort have flipped to the modern field direction, resulting in a dominant viscous remanence (\(\mathbf{M}_e\)).
Crucial physical distinction here: an individual single-domain grain doesn't have a '92% state.' It has two stable energy wells. It is either pointing one way or the other. The curve on the right tracks the statistical behavior of an entire *assemblage* of grains. (1) Point to the y-axis: At t=0, the entire population of grains is aligned pointing along Mo (ancient direction). (2) In the blue region (t << τ): The probability of any individual grain flipping is extremely low. The assemblage successfully preserves the stable remanence. (3) We've dropped the transition line at t=τ because relaxation is a smooth continuum. Instead, focus on the end states. (4) In the orange region (t > 2.5τ): Over 92% of the grains in this cohort have now thermally flipped. The collective memory of this cohort is effectively wiped, replaced by today's modern field direction (Me). This is the unwanted viscous remanence overprint we must identify and strip away in the lab.

VRM in the wild

  • Every ancient rock has spent the last ~780 kyr in the Brunhes normal-polarity field — so a present-field VRM overprint on top of any ancient remanence can be expected
  • VRM is carried by the lowest-stability grains — the same grains that are easiest to demagnetize (this is why thermal and AF demagnetization works to remove VRM)
  • Higher temperature → shorter \(\tau\) → faster viscous acquisition: burial at depth accelerates overprinting (thermoviscous remanence, next slide)
expected NRM of a Duluth Complex specimen: ancient TRM (1.1 Ga) + Brunhes-age VRM (< 0.78 Myr) — a two-component magnetization
The 0.78 Myr figure: the last reversal (Matuyama–Brunhes) — any VRM acquired before it points the other way and has mostly been overwritten since. This slide plants the two-component expectation that the Zijderveld part of the session resolves. Lab point: VRM acquired sitting on the bench in the Earth's field for a week is measurable in soft specimens.

Thermoviscous remanence: time and temperature trade off

  • The same \(Kv/k_BT\) exponent can be reached by long time at low T or short time at high T
  • Burial to a few hundred °C for Myr can remagnetize what a lab oven resets in minutes at higher T — read the nomogram along a curve
  • Conversely: a short lab heating cleans a long, low-T thermoviscous overprint
How to read: pick a point (say 250 °C, 10 Myr) and follow its curve down to the lab timescale (100 s, right axis) — a 10 Myr burial at 250 °C unblocks in the oven within a few tens of degrees higher over lab times. If asked about hematite: its Hc ∝ Ms³ gives an Ms⁴ scaling and much steeper curves — hematite VRM is harder to acquire and to clean. This nomogram underlies "unblocking temperature spectra date the overprint" arguments. Regenerate the figure with images/pullaiah.py.

Part 3

Thermoremanent magnetization

Cooling lava

  • Above \(T_c\) (580 °C for magnetite): grains are paramagnetic — no remanent magnetization
  • Just below \(T_c\): \(\tau\) is seconds — a biased dynamic equilibrium with the field
  • At the blocking temperature \(T_b\): \(\tau\) shoots past lab/geologic timescales — the biased distribution is locked in
  • The result: a TRM parallel to the field,
    (and proportional to it in weak fields)
Key subtlety: blocking happens tens to hundreds of degrees below the Curie temperature, grain by grain — Tb is a property of each grain (its v and K), not of the mineral. That's what makes partial TRM and thermal demagnetization possible.

Cooling changes both numerator and denominator

\(\tau = \dfrac{1}{C}\exp\left(\dfrac{Kv}{k_B T}\right)\)
  • Denominator ↓ (Thermal energy drops): As \(T\) falls, \(k_B T\) shrinks. Thermal agitation decreases, lowering capacity to cross energy barriers.
  • Numerator ↑ (Magnetic barrier grows): \(M_s\) is higher at lower \(T\). \(K \propto M_s^2\), such that energy barrier (\(Kv\)) amplifies quadratically.
  • During cooling, the relaxation time \(\tau\) skyrockets from seconds to geological timescales over a very narrow temperature interval, blocking the remanence.
First establish the denominator: as T falls, k_B T shrinks, so thermal agitation loses the capacity to hop the barrier. Next slide adds the numerator growing quadratically via K ∝ Ms².

This is the two-part mechanism the TRM widget animates: lowering T shrinks the denominator while simultaneously expanding the numerator quadratically. If asked why K ∝ Ms² for shape anisotropy: the demagnetizing energy is ½μ0ΔN Ms². For magnetocrystalline-dominated grains the exponent differs, but the qualitative story is identical.

Part 4

Chemical remanent magnetization

blocking by growing

Grow through the blocking volume

Hold the Néel equation at room \(T\) and solve for the volume where \(\tau\) reaches a target:

\(v_b = \dfrac{k_B T \ln(C\tau)}{K_u}\)
  • A crystal growing in the ambient field passes through \(v_b\): its biased moment distribution is blocked in at that size
  • No heat required — this happens during weathering, diagenesis, and alteration
Same picture, third slider. Point out the tiny scale of blocking volumes: tens of nm — a growing hematite or magnetite crystal acquires its CRM long before it is visible petrographically.

Watch CRM block in

  • Drag the growth slider (or press Grow): hematite grains nucleate small and grow at constant temperature
  • The \(\tau\) contours stay put — it's the population that rises through them as volume grows
  • Grains crossing \(\tau = 100\) s become blocked CRM carriers, recording the field at the time of their growth — not the rock's formation age

Interactive from Tauxe & Swanson-Hysell (2026), Essentials of Paleomagnetism, ch. 7 (CC BY 4.0).

Contrast with the TRM widget: there the curves moved and the grains slid sideways; here everything is at room temperature and only volume changes. Two CRM flavors to name: grain-growth CRM (gCRM — precipitation of new minerals) and alteration CRM (aCRM — one mineral replacing another, e.g. magnetite oxidizing to maghemite, where the new phase can inherit a memory of the old one's remanence).

CRM in the wild — signal and menace

  • CRM leads to overprints: oxidation, hydrothermal fluids, and serpentinization create new magnetic phases carrying the field of alteration time
  • Red beds: hematite CRM can be acquired soon (but how soon?) after deposition
The interpretive point: a CRM is a faithful field recorder — of the wrong (or at least a different) time. Whether that is signal or noise depends on the question. For the ultramafic D-6A units, serpentinite-hosted magnetite would carry a Phanerozoic-to-recent CRM wildly discordant from the 1.1 Ga TRM — a testable prediction for the group presentations.

Part 5

The NRM — and how we collect it

NRM: whatever nature left there

\(\mathrm{NRM} = \) vector sum of every remanence the rock has acquired and retained: TRM + VRM + CRM (+ IRM + …)

A Duluth Complex troctolite likely carries:

  • TRM from initial cooling at ca. 1096 Ma
  • VRM from the Brunhes chron (steep, north, down)
  • possibly CRM where serpentinized or oxidized
  • possibly drilling-induced IRM

The good news:

  • The components differ in stability (τ ↔ coercivity ↔ \(T_{ub}\))
  • Progressive demagnetization peels them apart, least stable first
Halfway summary slide — good place for the first long break. The second half is the reading half: sampling and orientation, measurement, demagnetization, Zijderveld analysis, and what direction we expect the D-6A core to carry. The field tests that establish a component's relative age are in the extras at the end. IRM and ARM in the sum get their formal definitions after the demagnetization part; for now "lightning and lab fields" is enough.

Getting oriented samples

  • Orientation on the outcrop: azimuth + plunge of the core axis (magnetic and/or sun compass + inclinometer)
  • Sample fresh rock: weathering trades magnetite for oxyhydroxides — a CRM factory
  • Check for lightning (compass sweep)
  • Precision improves as \(\sqrt{N}\): several samples per site, sites spread over time (secular variation!)
Site design logic from ch. 9: a site = one instant of geologic time (a cooling unit, one horizon). To average secular variation you need many sites, not many samples in one site — rule of thumb from the GAD chapter: tens of sites over >10⁵ yr. Sun compass matters on magnetite-rich outcrops (like most of the Duluth Complex!) where local anomalies deflect a magnetic compass; back-sighting is the quick check.

Sample, specimen, site — and their arrows

Terminology that saves grief: a sample is something you take; a specimen is something you measure. Multiple specimens per sample, multiple samples per site. Every lab has its own arrow convention — what matters is an unambiguous chain from specimen x,y,z to geographic coordinates. This is exactly the orient.txt / samples.txt bookkeeping in MagIC that the Part 8 data figure uses.

Measuring the vector — and putting it in coordinates

  • Spinner magnetometer: spin the specimen in a pickup coil; the induced emf gives two components per spin axis
  • Cryogenic (SQUID) magnetometer: superconducting pickup loops count flux quanta — fast, sensitive to ~10⁻¹¹ Am²; the IRM's 2G systems (incl. the RAPID) are this type
  • Either way you get specimen-coordinate components \((x, y, z)\):
\(x = M\cos I \cos D,\quad y = M\cos I \sin D,\quad z = M\sin I\)
  • Rotate by the sample's azimuth & plunge → geographic (\(x\) = north, \(y\) = east, \(z\) = down)
  • Untilt by strike & dip when the question calls for pre-tilt coordinates
Conventions: D measured clockwise from north, I positive down (northern hemisphere normal field: D≈0, I>0). The tilt correction is three rotations (rotate dip direction to north, untilt about horizontal, rotate back) or one composite rotation — PmagPy's pmag.dogeo / pmag.dotilt do exactly this, and the Part 7 Duluth Complex interactive uses dogeo on the real MagIC tables. Mass/volume normalization: moment (Am²) ÷ volume = A/m, as in S1. If the room wants to know how a SQUID actually works, the instrumentation deep-dive — flux quantization, shielding, sensor offsets — is in the extras at the end.

Part 6

Taking the NRM apart: demagnetization

The logic: erase in order of stability

  • Secondary components (VRM, lightning IRM) live in low-stability grains: short \(\tau\) ↔ low coercivity ↔ low unblocking temperature
  • Ancient components live in high-stability grains — that's why they're still there
  • So: progressively randomize the low-stability fraction, measuring the surviving vector after each step

Thermal demag

  • Heat to \(T\), cool in zero field
  • Randomizes all grains with \(T_{ub} < T\)
  • Selects on unblocking temperature

Alternating-field (AF) demag

  • Oscillating field, smoothly decaying, in zero DC field
  • Randomizes all grains with coercivity below the peak
  • Selects on coercivity

Two different axes through the same Néel diagram — which is why thermal and AF treatment of companion specimens can resolve components that either alone cannot.

The FC1 site in Part 8 is a perfect illustration — same sample, one specimen AF, one thermal, same ChRM recovered along different demag paths. Stability ordering is a tendency, not a law: a CRM can be hard, young hematite pigment can out-stubborn an old magnetite TRM in AF. Hence companion treatments.

Thermal demagnetization: unblocking the Néel diagram

  • Heat to \(T\): every grain with \(T_{ub} < T\) goes superparamagnetic
  • Cool in zero field: they re-block randomized — their remanence is erased
  • Reciprocity (SD): this removes exactly the pTRMs below \(T\)
  • A time–temperature trade (Pullaiah curves): minutes at 300 °C clean a VRM acquired over ~1 Myr at ambient temperature
  • Caution: heating in air can create new minerals (aCRM in the oven!) — monitor susceptibility between steps
This figure is the Néel-diagram version of what the TRM widget showed in reverse. If a stray field leaks into the cooling chamber, the specimen acquires a pTRM each step — the double-demag protocols (heat and cool twice, antiparallel positions) detect this. Alteration monitoring: kappa checks between steps; a rising susceptibility above ~400 °C usually means new magnetite from silicate/sulfide breakdown.

Run the cooling widget backwards: unblocking

  • Same interactive as the TRM part — now drag the temperature slider up, as the oven does
  • The \(\tau\) curves sweep up and the grain population slides left: grains drop below the \(\tau = 100\) s boundary and unblock, lowest \(T_{ub}\) first
  • Cooled back in zero field, each unblocked grain re-freezes with random polarity — its remanence is gone
  • Step to progressively higher \(T\) and you erase the blocking-temperature spectrum from the bottom up

Interactive from Tauxe & Swanson-Hysell (2026), Essentials of Paleomagnetism, ch. 7 (CC BY 4.0).

Deliberate reprise of the TRM widget: acquisition and demagnetization are the same physics traversed in opposite directions. Demo: start at 20 °C (fully blocked), step the slider to ~300 °C and point out which part of the ellipse has gone superparamagnetic — those are the grains a 300 °C oven step wipes (in zero field). Keep stepping to 560 °C: everything unblocks below the Curie point. Reciprocity caveat from the pTRM slide applies: for SD grains T_ub = T_b; multidomain grains unblock over a smeared range.

AF demagnetization: the waveform

  • While the envelope exceeds a grain's coercivity, its moment flips with every half-cycle
  • Once the envelope decays below that coercivity, the field can no longer switch the moment — it is left in the state set by the last half-cycle that could. That phase differs grain to grain, so half end up one way, half the other: net zero
  • Applied along three orthogonal axes (or while tumbling) so all easy-axis orientations are treated
  • Only grains with coercivity above the peak keep their memory
Stress: zero ambient DC field is essential — any bias field turns the procedure into ARM acquisition (formally defined in the next part). The smooth decay matters too: an abrupt cutoff leaves grains coherently stuck. The RAPID does 3-axis AF automatically; peak fields to 100–180 mT reach most magnetite, but not hematite (coercivities to several T — thermal territory).

Part 7

Seeing the components: Zijderveld diagrams

Progressive demagnetization, in 3-D

  • Every demagnetization step leaves a full vector — direction and intensity
  • Levels 1–3: a low-stability component (dashed arrow) is stripped — the vector changes direction and length
  • Levels 4–6: the surviving high-stability component decays in intensity but keeps its direction — heading straight for the origin
Scenario slide — let it breathe. The two-component story is obvious in 3-D: the vector swings while the overprint is stripped (1–3), then marches straight at the origin (4–6). The problem: 3-D is unplottable on paper. The next slide shows the solution — project it onto two orthogonal planes.

From 3-D to paper: project the endpoints

  • (a) orthogonal projection of the endpoints onto the horizontal plane — the map view
  • (b) projection onto a vertical (N–S) plane
  • (c) stack both projections on shared axes → one plot: the vector component (Zijderveld) diagram
Walk the construction slowly — this is the make-or-break slide for reading every demag figure that follows. Panel (a): looking straight down, the swing-then-straight path. Panel (b): looking sideways at the N–S plane, the inclination story. Panel (c): the space-saving trick — both projections on one pair of shared axes, distinguished only by symbol fill. One pair of points per demagnetization step.

Reading a vector component diagram

  • Solid symbols — horizontal projection (\(x_1\) vs \(x_2\)); open symbols — vertical projection (\(x_1\) vs \(x_3\), down positive): Zijderveld (1967)
  • Removing a single component of fixed direction moves the endpoint along a straight line
  • The last (characteristic) component, ChRM, decays to the origin
  • Straight segments = separable components; curvature = overlapping stability spectra
  • Each segment lies along the direction of the component being removed — but read it from the later step back toward the earlier one (away from the origin). The endpoint travels the opposite way, because each step subtracts that component

Equal-area projections complement the Zijderveld view: they show the direction of the surviving vector migrating as components strip away.

Be explicit about the sign, because it trips people up every year. The plotted point is the tip of what SURVIVES, so the move from step i to step i+1 is M(i+1) − M(i) — minus the component just removed. The path therefore runs toward the origin while the component itself points away from it. The easiest sanity check to give the room: on the final segment the surviving magnetization IS the ChRM, so its direction is simply the direction from the origin out to the plotted point — and the segment is collinear with that. This is also why PCA routines must fix the eigenvector sign by hand: the orientation tensor gives an axis, not a direction, so the code orients it from the late steps back toward the early ones. Warn about the classic trap: plotting H = √(x1²+x2²) vs x3 ("component plot") looks like a Zijderveld vertical projection but is NOT a linear projection — the apparent coordinate system changes at every step. Also worth mentioning once: some plots (PmagPy and this deck's interactives among them) rotate the horizontal axes so x1 lies along the NRM declination — it keeps both projections well spread out, and the vertical projection then shows an apparent inclination. Solid = map view, open = section view is the convention in the widget coming up.

The effect of overlapping spectra

  • The vector-addition view: at every step the plotted point is origin→B plus B→NRM (the A part)
  • With overlap, both shrink at once — the path bows between the two directions
  • Strong overlap: no segment is pure; a line fit anywhere is biased
  • Remedies: the other demag method (different spectra!), great-circle fits, or both
This static figure is the same physics students just explored — good recap anchor. Mention the vector difference sum (VDS): summing the step-to-step vector differences "straightens out" the total magnetization and gives an honest scalar decay for mdf purposes when components overlap.

Pairing thermal and AF demag for insight

  • Same core sample, one specimen AF, one thermal
  • Low — <10 mT, <150 °C: small VRM
  • Medium — 10–60 mT, 515–565 °C: primary TRM in magnetite → the pole
  • High — past 130 mT, but 250–350 °C: CRM in pyrrhotite
  • Higher coercivity, lower unblocking temperature.
The point of the slide: a single demagnetization experiment gives you components, but a PAIR of experiments on sister specimens gives you carriers. Walk the AF panel left to right first — three segments, three colors — then the thermal panel and ask the room to predict the unblocking order before revealing it. The surprise is that the high-coercivity (AF-hard) component dies at 250–350 °C, which is pyrrhotite's Curie temperature, while the medium-coercivity component survives to 515–565 °C, which is magnetite. Coercivity is about the energy barrier's height at room temperature; unblocking temperature is about how fast that barrier collapses on heating — different physics, so the orderings need not match. The AF decay panel is also where to define the mdf, the median destructive field that halves the NRM (~30 mT here) — a coarse stability index. Interpretation from the paper: the magnetite TRM is primary at ca. 1780 Ma and carries the pole; the pyrrhotite CRM formed during hydrothermal alteration associated with ca. 1096 Ma Midcontinent Rift magmatism, and its direction matches a Rift-age dike in the same area — a secondary magnetization you can date by its direction. Note this is our own data, so it is exactly the workflow the groups will run on the RAPID.

From directions to a paleomagnetic pole

  • A direction is local. Convert each site's direction + location into the pole position it implies under a geocentric axial dipole — its VGP — and the result is comparable between continents
  • Averaging VGPs averages away secular variation; the scatter that remains sets \(A_{95}\)
  • The pole falls between the 1100 and 1095 Ma positions on the Rift path — an independent check that the complex is ca. 1096 Ma
The conceptual jump of the session: directions are local, poles are global. Two sites on the same continent recording the same field give different declinations and inclinations but the SAME VGP — that is the whole point of the transformation, and it rests entirely on the GAD assumption. Averaging VGPs rather than directions is the convention because the VGP distribution is closer to Fisherian. Note the filter in the caption: only sites with α95 < 15°, and only one of the AF/thermal pair per sample, so N = 24 rather than every site. Right panel is the payoff — the pole lands between the 1100 and 1095 Ma positions of the independently dated volcanic pole path, which is how a paleomagnetic pole becomes a dating tool.

Reconstructing Laurentia from that pole

The logic in one sentence: a paleomagnetic pole is where the spin axis was *relative to the plate*, so putting the pole back on the spin axis puts the plate back where it was. It is a single Euler rotation — about an axis on the equator at (pole longitude − 90°) = 98.7°E, through (90° − pole latitude) = 54.4°. Watch Duluth: 46.8°N today, 31.9°N at 1096 Ma, matching the ~32°N the paper quotes. That is the tropical Midcontinent Rift. Two caveats worth saying out loud. (1) Paleolongitude is NOT constrained — the reconstruction can slide freely east-west, so only latitude and the continent's azimuthal orientation mean anything; the longitude you see is a display convention. (2) The polarity ambiguity: a pole and its antipode fit the data equally well, so choosing northern over southern hemisphere is an interpretation informed by other poles. Ask the room what a second pole of a different age would add — that is the apparent polar wander path, and the right panel of the previous slide is exactly that path.

IRM and ARM: remanence from applied fields

Isothermal remanent magnetization (IRM)

apply a field at a set temperature (usually room temperature)

  • Pulse at constant temperature in a field bigger than a grain's coercivity — the moment flips and stays. That's an IRM
  • Repeat with bigger pulses → the acquisition curve, whose rise traces the coercivity spectrum
  • It flattens at sIRM (\(M_r\)) — the most remanence the sample can hold
Contrast with the last three parts: TRM needed cooling, VRM needed time, CRM needed grain growth. IRM needs none of them — the field simply overwhelms the barrier, which is why "isothermal" is in the name. It is also the least interesting remanence to find in a rock and the easiest to make in a lab. IRM is the remanence of the hysteresis loop they measured on the VSM — the zero-field intercept after saturation, so sIRM here and Mr there are the same quantity. In the lab it is a workhorse: coercivity spectra, S-ratios, unmixing — later in the school. In nature it means one thing above all: lightning, next slide. If asked about the reverse experiment: applying a backfield and walking the remanence down through zero defines the coercivity of remanence, Hcr — useful for Day-plot style domain-state diagnostics, but that belongs with hysteresis rather than here.

Natural IRM: lightning

  • A strike delivers an intense, brief field (line current ~3 × 10⁵ A) that IRM-overprints everything within meters
  • Lightning-struck outcrops: erratic, strong NRMs — often soft (removed by modest AF), but they can obliterate the record
  • Field practice: walk the outcrop with a compass — deflections of > 5° at ~15 cm flag struck patches; sample elsewhere

Drilling itself can impart a (weak) IRM — another reason the drill-string is kept demagnetized for core studies like D-6A.

Fun demo if time: the compass-deflection trick is a genuinely useful field skill — high-standing knobs and ridge crests are the usual victims. Note that lightning IRM is usually carried by low-coercivity grains, so AF demagnetization often recovers the underlying TRM — the stability-ordering logic of the demagnetization part in action.

Anhysteretic remanent magnetization

  • Decaying AF plus a small DC bias (≈ 50–100 μT): as each grain's coercivity is passed, it freezes biased by the DC field instead of randomly — an ARM
  • An analog of TRM with the AF playing the role of temperature — without the chemistry-altering heat
  • ARM acquisition: hold the AF peak fixed (say 100 mT) and step the bias up, measuring each time — the IRM-acquisition idea with the bias field as the variable. In weak fields it is linear, and its slope is \(\chi_{ARM}\)
  • pARM: bias only over a coercivity window — the AF analog of pTRM
ARM is considered a TRM analog that can be imparted without heating can impart without heating — no thermochemical alteration, so the same specimen can be given it over and over. It preferentially activates fine, SD-size grains, which makes \(\chi_{ARM}\) and ARM/sIRM workhorse grain-size and concentration proxies; and it stands in for TRM where TRM is unavailable — normalizing NRM for relative paleointensity in sediments, and in pseudo-Thellier and Shaw-type paleointensity methods.
ARM matters here for three reasons: (1) it is the physics of the AF demag widget from the previous part — demagnetization is ARM acquisition with zero bias field; any stray DC field in the demag coil imparts an unwanted ARM; (2) χARM is a staple of environmental magnetism; (3) it is the practical substitute for TRM. Emphasize why that substitution matters: heating a sediment or a sulfide-bearing rock to give it a TRM often destroys the very mineral you are trying to measure, and you only get one shot. ARM is imparted at room temperature on the same AF equipment used for demagnetization, so it is repeatable and non-destructive — that is the whole reason the pseudo-Thellier and relative-paleointensity literature is built on it. The caveat worth stating: ARM is only a proxy for TRM. The two have different coercivity dependence and the ARM/TRM ratio depends on grain size and domain state, so relative paleointensity is relative, not absolute. GRM, the other remanence AF treatment can impart, has its own slide in the extras.

Additional material

Best-fit lines: principal component analysis

  • Choose the steps that define a component; build the orientation tensor of the (centered) endpoints
  • Eigenvectors \(\mathbf{V}_1, \mathbf{V}_2, \mathbf{V}_3\) with eigenvalues \(\tau_1 \ge \tau_2 \ge \tau_3\)
  • Best-fit line = \(\mathbf{V}_1\) (free, or anchored to the origin); best-fit plane (great circle) = the plane ⊥ \(\mathbf{V}_3\)
  • Quality: the maximum angle of deviation,
\(\mathrm{MAD}_{\mathrm{line}} = \tan^{-1}\!\sqrt{(\tau_2 + \tau_3)/\tau_1}, \qquad \mathrm{MAD}_{\mathrm{plane}} = \tan^{-1}\!\sqrt{\tau_3/\tau_2 + \tau_3/\tau_1}\)
  • Plus DANG, the angle between the line and the origin direction
  • Kirschvink (1980) — the universal standard (PmagPy: pmag.domean, Demag GUI, zeq)
  • Rules of thumb: fit ≥ 4 steps; MAD ≲ 5–10° for a line you'd defend; inspect DANG before calling it ChRM
  • Component directions → Fisher site means → poles (tomorrow's statistics)
The Zijderveld explorer widget in Part 7 computes exactly this (free line) — students have already seen MAD respond to overlap. Equation form as in Essentials ch. 9. Anchored vs free fits: anchor only when the segment demonstrably heads to the origin (small DANG); otherwise you bias the direction. Great-circle-plus- line combined site means: McFadden & McElhinny approach, mentioned for the curious.

The fold test

  • Demagnetization orders components by stability — field tests bound their age
  • The fold (tilt) test: does untilting the beds improve the grouping?
  • Positive → remanence acquired before folding
Statistics of "improves the grouping" (parameter comparisons, bootstrap fold tests) belong to the statistics sessions — here it's the logic that matters. Synfolding magnetizations show best grouping at partial untilting; that result is real and diagnostic, not a failure of the test.

The conglomerate test

  • Clasts were reoriented at deposition — whatever remanence they carried was randomized with them
  • Random clast directions → the clast lithology's remanence predates deposition, and the region was not remagnetized since
  • Clustered clast directions → everything was remagnetized after the conglomerate formed — a negative test
  • Note what is being tested: the age of the remanence in the clasts, relative to the conglomerate
The cleanest logic of all the field tests, and the easiest to state: if the clasts were tumbled into place, their directions must be random unless something reset them afterward. Randomness here is a statistical claim — testing it properly (uniformity tests on a directional distribution) is a statistics-session topic. Watch for the intraformational case, where clasts were reworked from still-soft sediment, and for clasts too big to have been fully rotated.

The baked-contact test

  • An intrusion bakes its host: rock at the contact is heated above its blocking temperatures and acquires the intrusion's direction
  • Walk away from the contact and the direction should grade back to the host rock's own, older direction
  • That gradient is the test: it shows the region was not wholesale remagnetized after the intrusion cooled
  • An inverse baked-contact test flips the roles: a younger cross-cutting intrusion tests an older target remanence — reset toward the intrusion at the contact, grading back to the older unit's own direction away from it
  • It shows the older remanence predates the cross-cutter — an older-than bound, not proof of acquisition at emplacement
The aristocrat of the tests for Precambrian work, and the Midcontinent Rift literature leans on it heavily. The gradient is what carries the argument — a match at the contact alone proves nothing, since a regional overprint would also produce one. For D-6A: the Archean Giants Range Batholith footwall at the bottom of the hole is a natural baked-contact conversation. Does the granite adjacent to the South Kawishiwi intrusion carry the 1.1 Ga direction, and does it grade back to something Archean away from the contact? A good stretch question for the felsic group. Keep the two directions of inference straight. The normal test asks whether the region escaped later wholesale remagnetization, using the intrusion's own direction as the known quantity. The inverse test runs the other way: a younger cross-cutting body is the tool, and the older unit's remanence is what's on trial. Passing it brackets the older remanence as pre-dating the cross-cutter — an older-than bound, not a demonstration that the remanence is primary at the emplacement age. The NWD1–NED17 result is described exactly this way in Swanson-Hysell et al. (2021), doi:10.1029/2021TC006751.

Gyroremanent magnetization (GRM)

  • Some specimens acquire a remanence during AF treatment rather than losing one — a gyroremanence, growing perpendicular to the last AF axis
  • Its signature is unmistakable on the decay curve: intensity that rises at high AF steps, with directions swinging toward that perpendicular axis
  • Characteristic of magnetically anisotropic specimens — greigite above all, and some hematite
  • The fix is the three-axis (triple-demagnetization) protocol: demagnetize along x, then y, then z, measuring after each, and combine them with the Dankers–Zijderveld / Stephenson algebra — not a simple vector average of the three
  • It assumes the induced GRM is perpendicular to the applied AF axis, and can fail for strongly GRM-producing specimens
Rising intensity at high AF is a lab-induced remanence, not a recovered signal.
  • Why it matters: mistaken for a "high-coercivity component", GRM invents a direction that never existed in the rock
  • Greigite-bearing sediments are the classic case — worth suspecting whenever AF demagnetization misbehaves above ~60 mT, and greigite can acquire GRM partly parallel to the treatment axis, breaking the correction's assumption
  • If GRM is present and cannot be cancelled, thermal demagnetization is the alternative — different physics, different artifacts
This slide teaches healthy paranoia. Students are trained to read a Zijderveld as a story of removal, so intensity that climbs is genuinely surprising the first time — make them look at the decay panel, not just the vector plot. GRM attribution as carried in this deck: Stephenson (1981) for the gyroremanence itself; Dankers & Zijderveld (1981), doi:10.1016/0012-821X(81)90029-7 and Stephenson (1993), doi:10.1029/92JB01849 for the three-axis correction algebra; Hu et al. (1998), doi:10.1046/j.1365-246X.1998.00627.x for the greigite case where GRM is not purely perpendicular to the treatment axis and the correction breaks down. Stress that the three sequential measurements represent three different stages of progressive demagnetization, so averaging the full vectors is not the right operation — the published algebra is. Greigite's association with GRM is why it comes up in environmental magnetism. Connect it back to ARM: both are remanences the AF treatment gives you rather than takes away, which is why zero DC field and clean waveforms matter so much.

Inside a superconducting rock magnetometer

the SQUID counts flux quanta

Schematic cross-section of a 2G-style SRM — the specimen translates along the bore through three orthogonal sensing regions.

  • A superconducting pickup loop rings the bore. Moving the specimen past it changes the enclosed flux, and because the loop has no resistance the induced supercurrent persists rather than decaying
  • That current is coupled into a SQUID: a superconducting ring interrupted by two Josephson junctions. It is a flux-to-voltage transducer — its voltage is periodic in the enclosed flux, one period per flux quantum
  • A flux-locked loop feeds back just enough current to null the flux — the feedback current is the reading, so the response stays linear over many decades
  • Coils are wound as gradiometers inside nested μ-metal and superconducting shields: a uniform ambient field cancels, a nearby dipole does not
  • Three coils at slightly offset positions along the bore give the full vector in one pass — seconds per specimen, no spinning
\(\Phi_0 = \dfrac{h}{2e} = 2.07\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{Wb}\)

Noise floor ~10⁻¹² Am² — orders of magnitude below a spinner, which is what puts weak sediments and single crystals in reach.

The key point: this is not a more sensitive coil, it is a different measurement. A spinner measures an emf, which scales with how fast you spin; a SQUID measures flux directly, so sensitivity does not depend on motion. Flux quantization in the superconducting loop sets the scale — Φ₀ = h/2e ≈ 2.07 × 10⁻¹⁵ Wb — and the SQUID resolves a small fraction of one quantum. Practical consequences worth flagging for the summer school: (1) the three sensing coils sit at different positions along the bore, which is why long-core and u-channel data need a sensor-offset correction and why the response function matters for deconvolution; (2) the shields make the bore a near-zero-field space, so specimens can sit inside without picking up a VRM during measurement; (3) a lightning-struck or otherwise very strong specimen can drive a flux jump — the FLL loses lock and the reading is off by an integer number of quanta, which is why you check for unreasonably large moments; (4) the IRM's RAPID system wraps this in an automated handler with in-line AF coils and ARM/IRM capability, so hundreds of demagnetization steps run unattended. Ask the group: given the noise floor here, what actually limits us on a weak sediment — the magnetometer, or the sample holder's own moment? (Usually the holder.)

How strong is a TRM? (Theory)

At the blocking temperature ($T_b$), a single domain's easy axes are populated according to Boltzmann statistics. For an individual grain oriented at an angle \(\theta\) to an applied magnetic field:

\(\dfrac{M(T_b)}{M_s} = \tanh\left(\dfrac{m B\cos\theta}{k_B T_b}\right)\)

To calculate the net remanence of a bulk rock sample, we integrate this response over a completely randomly oriented spatial assemblage of grains:

\(\dfrac{M_{TRM}}{M_s} = \int_0^{90^\circ} \tanh\left(\dfrac{m_o B\cos\theta}{k_B T_b}\right)\cos\theta\,\sin\theta \, d\theta\)
The tanh comes from the same Langevin-flavored competition between magnetic energy and thermal chaos seen in paramagnetism. Crucially, it is evaluated at Tb using the full grain moment (m = Ms·v), which is massive compared to a single atomic moment. This enormous volume multiplier is why TRM is incredibly efficient at locking in a signal, whereas equilibrium in-field magnetization at room temperature is comparatively feeble.

TRM Linearity & Paleointensity

  • The weak-field linear approximation: the argument of the hyperbolic tangent is small whenever
    \(m(T_b)\,B \ll k_B T_b \quad \rightarrow \quad \tanh x \approx x \quad \rightarrow \quad \mathbf{TRM \propto B}\)
    Most absolute TRM paleointensity methods assume or test this low-field proportionality.
  • Where linearity ends is grain-dependent: the condition involves \(m(T_b) = M_s(T_b)\,v\), so the range of linear behavior depends on grain volume, domain state and blocking temperature — there is no universal threshold field. Many suitable recorders are approximately linear across geomagnetic field strengths, but some silicate-hosted magnetite and vortex-state grains show appreciable nonlinearity even at tens of \(\mu\)T.
Panel (a) shows ideal single-domain theoretical curves flattening at high fields; panel (b) shows experimental points from natural specimens. Resist the temptation to read a single saturation field off these curves — the controlling ratio is m(T_b)B / k_B T_b, and m(T_b) = Ms(T_b)·v spans orders of magnitude across a natural grain population, so the field at which curvature becomes appreciable moves with grain volume, domain state and blocking temperature. Selkin, Gee & Tauxe (2007), doi:10.1016/j.epsl.2007.01.017 documented pronounced nonlinearity near geomagnetic field strengths in some natural materials — silicate-hosted magnetite in particular — which is precisely why good paleointensity practice tests the proportionality rather than assuming it.

How strong is a TRM?

At the blocking temperature, the two easy directions are populated by Boltzmann statistics; for a grain at angle \(\theta\) to the field:

\(\dfrac{M(T_b)}{M_s} = \tanh\dfrac{m B\cos\theta}{k_B T_b}\)

Integrated over a randomly oriented assemblage:

\(\dfrac{M_{TRM}}{M_s} = \int_0^{90^\circ} \tanh\left(\dfrac{m_o B\cos\theta}{k_B T_b}\right)\cos\theta\,\sin\theta \, d\theta\)
  • When \(m(T_b)B \ll k_B T_b\): \(\tanh x \approx x\) → TRM ∝ B — assumed or tested by most absolute TRM paleointensity methods
  • Because \(m(T_b) = M_s(T_b)\,v\), the linear range is grain- and blocking-temperature dependent — no universal threshold field. Many good recorders stay linear across geomagnetic field strengths; some silicate-hosted magnetite and vortex-state grains do not, even at tens of μT
The tanh is the same Langevin-flavored competition from S1's paramagnetism slide — but evaluated at Tb with the full grain moment m = Ms·v, which is enormous compared to a single atomic moment: that's why TRM is efficient while equilibrium in-field magnetization at room T is feeble. Paleointensity (Maxwell, Wed 7/29) rests on the weak-field proportionality — note that the condition m(T_b)B ≪ k_B T_b is a statement about the grain, not about a particular field value, so where a given specimen departs from linearity depends on its volume, domain state and blocking temperature. Selkin, Gee & Tauxe (2007), doi:10.1016/j.epsl.2007.01.017 found appreciable nonlinearity at geomagnetic field strengths in some natural materials.

Partial TRM: the spectrum is additive

  • A field applied only while cooling through \([T_1, T_2]\) magnetizes only the grains with \(T_b\) in that window: a pTRM
  • Additivity: \(\mathrm{TRM} = \sum_i \mathrm{pTRM}(T_{b_i})\)
  • Reciprocity: for SD grains, blocking and unblocking temperatures coincide (\(T_b = T_{ub}\)) — heat to \(T\) in zero field and you erase exactly the pTRMs below \(T\)
  • These two laws are the foundation of thermal demagnetization and of Thellier paleointensity
Flag the SD caveat: reciprocity fails for multidomain and vortex-state grains (unblocking spreads below the blocking interval), which is the chief headache of paleointensity work. A rock that cooled slowly can also have pTRMs reset by later reheating events — that's the TVRM story from Part 2 in pTRM language.

TRM depends (strongly) on grain size

  • Fine SD grains (tens of nm): efficient recorders
  • 1–10 μm grains: TRM an order of magnitude weaker (vortex/multidomain states) — yet still measurable and often stable
  • Typical basalt: TRM ≈ 1 A/m — strong; your Duluth Complex troctolites and gabbros carry NRMs in this ballpark

Domain states and their remanence: Maxwell's session (Tue) and the QDM station show which grains carry it.

Connect to D-6A: FC-site gabbros from the same magmatic system have NRMs of several A/m (the interactive data figure in Part 8). Oxide-rich units will be stronger still — but strength ≠ stability; the demag data will tell.
