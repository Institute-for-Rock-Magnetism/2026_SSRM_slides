Part 2

Fields, magnetization, and units

Where do magnetic fields come from?

moving electric charge—electric currents—and by the intrinsic spin and orbital motion of electrons.
  • Electron orbital motion and intrinsic spin in atoms → atomic magnetic moments — the source of every rock's magnetism
  • Electrons flowing in wires → electromagnets — the coils that apply fields in every instrument you will use this week
  • At the planetary scale: convecting currents in Earth's liquid iron outer core → the geomagnetic field
This single idea unifies the session: the same physics connects the coil in the VSM, the electrons in an olivine crystal, and the core dynamo. Everything that follows — moments, magnetization, susceptibility — is bookkeeping for electron currents and moments. One honest caveat if asked: spin is an intrinsic quantum property, not a literal spinning ball of charge — the "orbiting and spinning electron" is a cartoon, but the moments are real.

Three vector fields

  • Magnetic field strength \(\mathbf{H}\) — the applied (magnetizing) field, sourced by electric currents · A/m
  • Magnetization \(\mathbf{M}\) — magnetic moment per unit volume carried by matter · A/m
  • Magnetic induction \(\mathbf{B}\) (flux density) — the total field, drive + material response · tesla (T)
\(\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\,(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M})\),   with   \(\mu_0 \approx 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}\)

\(\mathbf{B}\) is what interacts with the physical world — it exerts the Lorentz force and induces voltages. A magnetometer senses the flux or field produced by a rock and reports its magnetic moment; dividing by volume or mass gives magnetization.

Geomagnetic fields are weak: we quote \(\mathbf{B}\) in μT (Earth's surface field ≈ 25–65 μT) and nT.

Where the units come from: a current loop

Field at the center of a loop of radius \(r\) carrying current \(i\):

\(H = \dfrac{i}{2r}\ \mathrm{[A/m]}\)
\(B = \mu_0 H = \dfrac{\mu_0 i}{2r}\) (in vacuum)

The loop has a magnetic moment:

\(m = i \cdot \mathrm{area}\ \mathrm{[A\,m^2]}\)

Magnetization: the volume density of magnetic dipoles

\(\mathbf{M} = \dfrac{1}{V}\sum_i \mathbf{m}_i\)

Sum the atomic dipole moments in a volume, divide by that volume — a vector density.

A uniformly magnetized body can be described equivalently as:

  • an assembly of current loops — interior currents cancel, leaving surface (Ampèrian) currents, like a solenoid
  • an assembly of magnetic dipoles — interior poles cancel, leaving surface poles on the ends

For a given magnetization the two descriptions are mathematically equivalent — they produce the same field. The loop picture is the physical one (electron currents), the pole picture is often the easier one to compute with.

Normalizing magnetic moment: magnetization

A measured moment \(m\ \mathrm{[A\,m^2]}\) scales with how much material is in the instrument. To compare specimens we normalize:

By volume

\(M = \dfrac{m}{V}\ \mathrm{[A/m]}\)
  • Same units as \(H\) — so \(\kappa = M/H\) is dimensionless
  • Natural for field comparisons (e.g. anomaly modeling) and volume susceptibility
  • Requires knowing the specimen volume

By mass

\(\sigma = \dfrac{m}{\mathrm{mass}}\ \mathrm{[A\,m^2/kg]}\)
  • Mass is quicker and more precise to measure than volume — chips, powders, irregular specimens
  • How VSM and MPMS data are usually reported (e.g. magnetite \(M_s \approx 92\ \mathrm{A\,m^2/kg}\))
  • Mass susceptibility \(\chi = \kappa/\rho\ \mathrm{[m^3/kg]}\)

You will deal with both conventions this week: every instrument fundamentally measures a moment-related signal, but Kappabridge susceptibilities are typically reported volume-normalized (dimensionless SI) while VSM and MPMS magnetizations are typically mass-normalized (A m²/kg). Converting between them just takes the density.

Two kinds of magnetization

Induced magnetization

  • Measured in an applied field
  • The reversible response — vanishes when the field is removed
  • Proportional to the field for most rocks in weak fields: \(\mathbf{M}_{ind} = \kappa\,\mathbf{H}\)
  • What a Kappabridge measures (low-field susceptibility)

Remanent magnetization

  • Persists in the absence of an external field — memory
  • Recorded when the rock formed (or was later remagnetized)
  • The signal paleomagnetism reads
  • What a magnetometer measures in zero field

A rock in Earth's field can carry both at once — their ratio in the ambient geomagnetic field is the Koenigsberger ratio \(Q = M_{rem}/M_{ind} = M_{rem}/\kappa H\), the quantity your project will pin down for each Duluth Complex unit, informing future aeromagnetic interpretation.

Part 3

Magnetic susceptibility

Susceptibility: the response coefficient

Place a material in a field \(\mathbf{H}\); it develops a magnetization \(\mathbf{M}\). For most geological materials in weak fields the response is linear:

\(\mathbf{M} = \kappa\,\mathbf{H}\)
  • \(M\) and \(H\) share units, so volume susceptibility \(\kappa\) is dimensionless (SI)
  • \(\kappa\) is an efficiency ratio: magnetization gained per unit of applied field
  • The sign and magnitude of \(\kappa\) are a window into atomic-scale magnetic behavior — and our first diagnostic of mineralogy

Three classes of magnetic response

Diamagnetic

κ < 0, small (~ −10⁻⁵)

Induced magnetization opposes the field. All matter does this.

Quartz, calcite, plagioclase, water

Paramagnetic

κ > 0, small (10⁻⁵–10⁻³)

Unpaired spins partially align with the field; thermal energy randomizes.

Olivine, pyroxene, amphibole, biotite

Ferromagnetic (s.l.)

κ ≫ 0, huge (up to ~10⁰–10¹)

Exchange coupling orders spins collectively → spontaneous magnetization + memory.

Magnetite, hematite, pyrrhotite

Ferromagnetic minerals are so strong that trace amounts of magnetite dominate the bulk susceptibility of a rock — which is why susceptibility can work as a proxy for magnetic mineral concentration.

Where common minerals sit

The D-6A along-core profile spans this entire range — from the nearly diamagnetic granophyre to the Fe-Ti oxide-rich U3 pods.

Diamagnetism

every atom does it — weakly, and backwards

  • The behavior of materials with no unpaired electrons — no permanent atomic moments, but orbiting electrons are still microscopic current loops
  • An applied field modifies the orbital motion (Larmor precession) → an induced moment that opposes the field
  • A direct consequence of electromagnetic induction — needs no permanent atomic moments
  • Nearly temperature independent: thermal agitation doesn't disrupt an induced orbital response
  • Present in all matter, but so weak that any para- or ferromagnetic contribution buries it

Quartz, calcite, feldspar → clean sandstones and limestones can have net negative susceptibility. κ ~ −10⁻⁶ to −10⁻⁵ (SI).

Explore: the diamagnetic response

Drag the field slider (or cycle it): induced moments grow with the field and always oppose it, so the M–B line has a negative slope. When B returns to zero, M returns to zero — no memory. (The slider reports the applied field as \(B = \mu_0 H\); the susceptibility relation remains \(M = \kappa H\).)

Start at zero field: nothing to see — diamagnetic moments are induced, not permanent. Drag B up slowly and watch the opposing moments grow; flip the field sign and the moments flip with it. Note the tiny magnitude: −4.8 A/m at 0.4 T.

Paramagnetism

permanent moments vs. thermal chaos

  • Atoms with unpaired electrons (Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺…) carry permanent magnetic moments — and two energies compete:

Magnetic energy — the field pulls each moment into alignment:

\(E_m = -\mu B\cos\theta\)

Thermal energy — randomizes the moments:

\(E_T = k_B T\),   \(k_B = 1.38\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\)

Example: one high-spin Fe²⁺ ion, as in fayalite, Fe₂SiO₄ (\(\mu \approx 5\mu_B\))

B, T\(\mu B\)\(k_B T\)\(\mu B / k_B T\)
B = 0.01 T, T = 300 K4.6 × 10⁻²⁵ J4.1 × 10⁻²¹ J10⁻⁴ \(\mu B \ll k_B T\) — weak alignment
B = 10 T, T = 10 K4.6 × 10⁻²² J1.4 × 10⁻²² J3 \(\mu B > k_B T\) — strong alignment
The energy budget frames everything: at Earth-like fields and room temperature the magnetic energy is four orders of magnitude below thermal — alignment is a tiny statistical bias, which is why κ is small and linear. Flip to MPMS conditions (10 T, 10 K) and μB beats k_BT — you can approach saturation of a paramagnet. That ratio μB/k_BT is exactly the Langevin argument.

Paramagnetism is strongly temperature dependent

thermal energy competes with alignment

  • Zero field: thermal agitation randomizes moments → no net magnetization
  • Applied field: partial alignment → small positive \(M\), linear in weak fields
  • Cool it down and the moments become more effectively aligned: κ grows as \(1/T\)
Curie's law: \(\kappa \approx \dfrac{C}{T}\)

The Curie constant \(C \propto N\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}^2\) encodes how many moments and how strong — in rocks it largely tracks the abundance of Fe-bearing ions.

linear response through the origin — no memory
cool it down and κ climbs as 1/T — Curie's law
Left: the whole paramagnetic response is a line through the origin — remove the field and M vanishes, no remanence. Right: read the curve toward low T — cooling quiets the thermal agitation, so the same field aligns the moments more effectively and κ climbs as 1/T. This is why low-temperature measurements (MPMS this week) are so sensitive to paramagnetic Fe. If asked: Curie's law is the ideal noninteracting limit — interacting moments follow Curie–Weiss, κ = C/(T−θ). Segue to the table of iron-bearing silicates next.

Explore: the paramagnetic response

Permanent moments jitter thermally and lean into the field — positive slope, ~100× steeper than the diamagnetic response. Raise T and watch the moments get wilder and κ fall (Curie's law). Still a line through the origin: no remanence, no coercivity, no memory.

Caveats: the alignment is greatly exaggerated for visibility (even at the slider extremes of 1 T and 100 K, μB/kBT ≈ 0.03, so a faithful drawing would look nearly random); the jitter is a schematic wobble, not Boltzmann statistics, such that the plotted M follows the Curie law with the arrows illustrating the mechanism but not being summed for the result.

Start at zero field: the moments exist (unlike the diamagnet) but point randomly — zero net M. Drag B up: they lean in. Then drag temperature from 300 K to 900 K at fixed B and watch M drop as κ ∝ 1/T. Key takeaway for both linear responses: when B returns to zero, M returns to zero — the "hysteresis loop" collapses to a line. That sets up ferromagnetism. How the visualization works: each orange arrow is the permanent moment of one atom — fixed length, because |μ| never changes; only its orientation responds. The wobble is a cartoon of thermal agitation (its amplitude and speed grow with T), and each moment is leaned from its random thermal direction toward B by a Langevin-like factor that saturates with B and falls as 1/T. The faint blue background arrows are the applied field, scaled to its strength. Simplifications to be upfront about: (1) the alignment is exaggerated ~10,000× — at these fields and temperatures μB/k_BT ~ 10⁻⁴ (previous slide's energy budget), so a faithful drawing would look completely random; (2) the jitter is a smooth deterministic wobble, not a real Boltzmann-statistics simulation — 24 moments in 2-D stand in for an ensemble average; (3) moments are independent (ideal paramagnet, no interactions); and (4) the plotted M and κ readout come from the exact Curie law κ = κ₃₀₀·(300/T), not from summing the drawn arrows — the arrows illustrate the mechanism, the curve is the physics.

Ferromagnetism (s.l.)

exchange coupling changes everything — and gives rocks memory

  • In dia- and paramagnets, atoms act independently
  • In ferromagnets (sensu lato), quantum-mechanical exchange interactions couple neighboring moments into collective long-range order
  • Spontaneous magnetization — magnetized even with no applied field
  • Hysteresis — magnetization depends on field history: rocks can remember

Exchange and superexchange in Earth materials

  • Magnetic order in rocks is controlled mainly by exchange interactions: how electron spins influence each other through the overlap of electron clouds between neighboring atoms
  • In oxides and sulfides, the magnetic (Fe) ions are separated by O²⁻ or S²⁻ anions
  • Coupling therefore usually occurs through the anion: superexchange
  • Superexchange commonly favors antiparallel alignment of neighboring spins

This antiparallel coupling is the basis for both antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism — the orderings that matter most in Earth materials.

In iron metal, direct exchange between neighboring atoms favors parallel spins — true ferromagnetism. In the minerals we care about, the dominant Fe–Fe coupling is mediated through the intervening oxygen (or sulfur) — superexchange — which commonly (though not invariably; it depends on bond geometry and orbital occupancy) favors antiparallel spins. Magnetite's B sublattice, with electron hopping between Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, is a more complicated story than the simple ionic picture. Still, the punchline holds: "ferromagnetic" minerals in rocks are almost all antiferromagnets in disguise — either canted (hematite) or with unbalanced sublattices (magnetite) — which the next slide makes concrete.

Why magnetite is ferrimagnetic

\(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4} \;=\; \mathrm{Fe^{3+}_{A}\,[Fe^{3+}Fe^{2+}]_{B}\,O_4}\)
  • Two magnetic sublattices: A (tetrahedral) sites and B (octahedral) sites
  • A sites hold only Fe³⁺; B sites hold Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺
  • Superexchange through the oxygens forces the A and B sublattices antiparallel
  • The Fe³⁺ moments on A and B cancel; the Fe²⁺ moment does not
  • Result (in this ideal ionic model): antiparallel order with a net magnetization ≈ the Fe²⁺ moment (~4 μB per formula unit)

Ferrimagnetism = antiparallel alignment + unequal moments.

This is the payoff of the superexchange slide: magnetite is not a ferromagnet in the strict sense — it is an antiferromagnet whose two sublattices don't balance. The bookkeeping: Fe³⁺ is 5 μB, Fe²⁺ is ~4 μB; A-site Fe³⁺ (down) cancels B-site Fe³⁺ (up), leaving the B-site Fe²⁺ ≈ 4 μB per formula unit — which matches the measured Ms of magnetite. Same logic, different arithmetic, gives hematite its much weaker canted moment.

Anatomy of a hysteresis loop

Ms — saturation magnetization

A strong enough field drives M to its high-field limit. ∝ concentration of ferromagnetic minerals.

Mr — saturation remanence

Remove the field and magnetization stays. This is why rocks record paleomagnetism.

Bc — coercivity

Reverse field needed to drive M back to zero — resistance to field-driven remagnetization, a key ingredient of remanence stability (along with grain size, domain state, and temperature).

Point at each feature on the loop as you define it: Ms at the closed high-field branches, Mr where the descending branch crosses B = 0, Bc where M passes through zero. On the Lake Shore VSM this week they will measure all three on their unit's specimens; loop shape vs. grain size gets its own session (fine-particle magnetism).

Explore: ferromagnetic hysteresis

Stoner–Wohlfarth physics: uniaxial single-domain magnetite grains with randomly oriented easy axes (shape anisotropy of prolate grains, BK = μ₀(Nb−Na)Ms). Each moment rides its energy minimum — rotating reversibly toward B, flipping irreversibly when the minimum vanishes. The loop is computed from these same grains: Mr and Bc are emergent, not dialed in.

Starts demagnetized: every moment on its own easy axis with random polarity, M = 0. Press cycle field: at peak field the moments approach alignment with B (that's Ms); back at zero each relaxes to the easy-axis direction nearest the field — that's Mr, and that's why rocks remember. Reverse the field to walk M through zero at Bc; each step in the loop is one grain flipping. Drag elongation: skinnier grains → higher B_K → wider loop — a preview of the anisotropy session. New grains re-draws the random easy axes.

Ferromagnetism is gone at the Curie temperature

  • Thermal energy vs. exchange: exchange wins at low T — long-range order below the ordering temperature, paramagnetic behavior above
  • Curie temperature Tc: loss of ferro/ferrimagnetic order · Néel temperature TN: loss of antiferromagnetic order
  • Magnetite: Tc ≈ 580 °C · hematite: TN ≈ 675 °C
  • The ordering temperature can be diagnostic → thermomagnetic runs on the Kappabridge furnace

Real rocks are mixtures

Magnetite is ~230× stronger than hematite (Ms 92 vs 0.4 Am²/kg). Slide the magnetite fraction: in this two-component model, near ~0.5 wt% the two phases contribute comparably and the loop goes wasp-waisted — a pinched loop is a classic sign of mixed soft + hard coercivity. Coercivity spectra and unmixing will be covered later on in the summer school.

Start at 0.5% — the classic wasp-waist. Slide down to 0.01%: hematite-dominated, broad loop. Slide up to a few %: pure-looking magnetite loop, hematite invisible. Lesson: hysteresis is exquisitely sensitive to trace strong phases — same reason bulk susceptibility is a magnetite-concentration proxy in D-6A.
