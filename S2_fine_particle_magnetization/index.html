Part 1

Electronic spins:
where atomic moments come from

Unpaired spins make atomic magnets

  • Each electron carries a spin moment of one Bohr magneton: \(m_b = 9.27\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{Am^2}\)
  • Electrons fill orbitals under three rules: Pauli exclusion, increasing energy, and Hund's rule
  • Paired spins cancel; unpaired spins in the 3d shells of transition metals survive as net atomic moments
Ion 3d electrons Unpaired spins Moment (spin-only)
Fe3+ 3d5 5 5 mb
Fe2+ 3d6 4 4 mb
Ti4+ 3d0 0 0

Iron is the star of rock magnetism — Mn2+ (5 mb) and Cr3+ (3 mb) also have magnetic moments but are less abundant on Earth.

Quick recap connecting to yesterday. The important thing to say out loud: transition metals lose the 4s electrons first when they ionize. So Fe3+ is 3d5 with five unpaired spins, and Fe2+ is 3d6 with four unpaired spins. The diagram isolates just the five 3d orbitals, which is why it is easier to read than a full neutral-atom filling diagram.

Paramagnetism: the Langevin function

independent moments vs. thermal chaos

  • No exchange coupling → each atomic moment responds independently to two competing influences:
    • the field, which pulls it into alignment (\(E_m = -mB\cos\theta\))
    • thermal agitation \(k_BT\), which randomizes it
  • The net alignment depends on the ratio of the two energies (Langevin, 1905):
\(\dfrac{M}{M_s} = \coth a - \dfrac{1}{a} = \mathcal{L}(a)\)
with  \(a = mB\,/\,k_B T\)

\(m\) — atomic magnetic moment (unpaired spins) · \(B\) — applied field · \(k_B\) — Boltzmann's constant · \(T\) — temperature (K) · \(M_s\) — saturation magnetization: every moment aligned

Keep this quick — S1 covered the phenomenology; this is the theory backbone. The core move: Boltzmann statistics on a two-energy competition, and the answer depends only on the dimensionless ratio a. Walk the figure: the initial slope is a/3 (next slide runs with it); saturation needs a ~ 10-20, which is ~900 T at 300 K even for a 5-Bohr-magneton ion — hence no lab saturates a paramagnet at room temperature. The Langevin function is today's recurring character: Weiss theory feeds it a molecular field to get ferromagnetism; superparamagnetism feeds it a whole particle moment instead of an atomic one.

Paramagnetism: Curie's law

  • Paramagnetic susceptibility (\(\chi_p\)) varies inversely with temperature. In Earth-like fields (where the response is linear), the relationship is:
\(\chi_p = \dfrac{N m^2 \mu_0}{3 k_B v}\cdot\dfrac{1}{T} = \dfrac{C}{T}\)

\(N/v\) — number of moments per volume · \(m\) — atomic moment · \(\mu_0\) — permeability of free space · \(C\) — the Curie constant, set by how many moments and how strong

  • \(\chi_p\) is positive, small, and ∝ 1/T — cool the specimen and the same field aligns the moments more effectively
  • Remove the field and \(M\) vanishes: no memory
Recall: magnetic susceptibility (\(\chi\)) is the response coefficient — the magnetization gained per unit of applied field, \(M=\chi H\)
Two ideas to land: (1) in weak fields everything is linear — this is why "susceptibility" is a useful single number for rocks; (2) the 1/T is a measurement lever: at MPMS temperatures (10-20 K) the paramagnetic response of Fe-bearing silicates is ~15-30x its room-temperature value, which is why low-T curves are so sensitive to the paramagnetic matrix. The Curie constant C encodes concentration x moment strength — in rocks it largely tracks Fe content. If asked about interacting moments: Curie-Weiss chi = C/(T - theta) is coming two slides ahead.

Part 2

Ferromagnetism:
spins working in concert

Exchange coupling creates magnetic order

Exchange couples neighboring spins through orbital overlap.

\(\epsilon_e = -2 J_e\, \mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_j\)

\(\epsilon_e\): exchange energy; \(J_e\): exchange constant, with units of energy; \(\mathbf{S}_i,\mathbf{S}_j\): spin vectors on neighboring lattice sites \(i\) and \(j\).

\(J_e>0\): parallel      \(J_e<0\): antiparallel
  • Direct exchange requires overlap between neighboring magnetic-ion orbitals and is important in Fe, Ni, and Co metals.
  • In oxides and sulfides, coupling commonly passes through an anion’s \(p\) orbitals: superexchange, often favoring antiparallel alignment.
  • Exchange energies are enormous: equivalent to a field of order 1000 T on each moment.
The 1000 T number is the key intuition: exchange is not a gentle bias like the Earth's field on a paramagnet — it is overwhelming, which is why spontaneous magnetization survives thermal agitation up to hundreds of °C. In the sign convention used here, positive J_e favors parallel alignment and negative J_e favors antiparallel alignment. Other texts may omit the factor of 2 or reverse the sign convention. Superexchange is often antiferromagnetic, especially for near-180-degree pathways between suitable occupied orbitals, but its sign also depends on orbital occupancy and bond geometry. The 100-to-1000 T scale is an equivalent internal exchange field, not a macroscopic field outside the specimen. It explains why magnetic ordering can survive thermal agitation to hundreds of degrees Celsius.

Antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism are the main ordering types in terrestrial rock-forming minerals — true ferromagnetism is for metals. Ordering vanishes above the Curie temperature (ferro/ferrimagnets) or Néel temperature (antiferromagnets).

Spin-ordering panels after R. Egli

Walk the zoo left to right: true ferromagnetism is essentially absent from the mineral world (iron metal — meteorites, the core); everything we measure this week is an antiferromagnet in disguise. Magnetite: unbalanced sublattices (recall the 5-and-4 Bohr magneton bookkeeping from S1). Hematite: canting by a fraction of a degree plus defect moments — which is why its Ms is ~230× weaker than magnetite's. The two hematite mechanisms (canting vs defect) behave differently at low temperature — the Morin transition suppresses canting but not the defect moment (Thursday's low-temperature lecture).

Ferromagnetic order dies at the Curie temperature

  • As temperature increases (\(T \rightarrow T_c\)), thermal fluctuations increasingly disrupt exchange alignment, causing saturation magnetization (\(M_s\)) to collapse.
  • Above \(T_c\), the mineral becomes paramagnetic, governed by the Curie–Weiss law (which accounts for exchange interactions between spins):
\(\chi = \dfrac{C}{T - \theta}\) θ = 0 — paramagnet (pure Curie law)
θ > 0 — ferromagnetic interactions
θ < 0 — antiferromagnetic interactions
  • Near \(T_c\), the collapse of order is characterized by a quantum-mechanical power law: \(\;M_s(T)/M_s(T_0) = \left[(T_c - T)/(T_c - T_0)\right]^{\gamma}\)

For magnetite, data (B. Moskowitz, in Banerjee 1991) are best fit with \(\gamma \approx 0.36\text{–}0.39\) and \(T_c \approx 565\text{–}580°\mathrm{C}\). The ordering temperature is diagnostic of mineralogy (more in Friday's high-T lecture).

We intentionally skip Weiss molecular field theory on this slide to focus on real physical behavior—Weiss is a mean-field theory that incorrectly predicts a critical exponent of 0.5, whereas real magnetite near Tc displays critical behavior with γ ≈ 0.36-0.39. Moskowitz's data were measured here at the IRM. Ms(T) matters beyond thermomagnetic runs: anisotropy constants scale as powers of Ms, so all of today's energy barriers are temperature dependent — the seed of thermal demagnetization and TRM (tomorrow's session). Notation footnote if asked: we follow the Essentials chapter in calling the Ms(T) exponent γ; the critical-phenomena literature writes this magnetization exponent as β and reserves γ for susceptibility. Either way it is an asymptotic law near Tc, not a whole-range description.

Part 3

Anisotropy:
why some directions are easy

The governing principle: energy minimization

A ferromagnetic grain adopts the magnetization configuration that minimizes its total energy. The key energies (as volume-normalized energy densities \(\epsilon\), J/m³) are:

Exchange \(\epsilon_e\)

Strongly favors neighboring spins remaining aligned, but does not by itself select a preferred direction in the grain.

Zeeman Energy (External Field) \(\epsilon_z = -\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{\text{ext}}\)

Pulls M toward an applied field — the energy that lets fields do work on the grain. Its self-field sibling — the demagnetizing (self) energy — appears under shape anisotropy.

Anisotropy \(\epsilon_a\)

Defines energetically favorable ("easy") and unfavorable ("hard") axes of magnetization within the grain. Driven by magnetocrystalline (lattice symmetry), magnetostrictive (stress), and shape (demagnetizing field) effects

Anisotropy creates energy barriers between "easy" directions. A barrier high enough to resist thermal fluctuations can hold a magnetization in place for billions of years — the physical basis of paleomagnetism.

This slide is the thesis of the whole session. Everything that follows is computing ε_a for different mechanisms, then asking when thermal energy or an applied field can push M over the barrier. Note the convention: ε for energy density (J/m³), E for energy (J) — the barrier that matters for stability is ε times the grain volume, which is why grain SIZE is everything in rock magnetism.

Magnetocrystalline anisotropy: magnetite

For a cubic crystal, with direction cosines \(\alpha_i\) of M relative to [100], [010], [001]:

\(\epsilon_a = K_1(\alpha_1^2\alpha_2^2 + \alpha_2^2\alpha_3^2 + \alpha_3^2\alpha_1^2) + K_2\,\alpha_1^2\alpha_2^2\alpha_3^2\)
  • Magnetite at 300 K:
    \(K_1 = -1.35\times10^4\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\)
  • \(K_1 < 0\) → easy along [111] body diagonals, hard along [100] cube edges
The 3d orbitals "poke" in particular crystallographic directions, so spin–orbit coupling makes the exchange-coupled spin system feel the lattice. The empirical constants K1, K2 package all of that. Easy = [111] is specific to magnetite at room temperature — and it CHANGES with temperature, which is the next slide and the interactive.

Anisotropy constants depend strongly on temperature

  • Electronic interactions depend on interatomic spacing → \(K_1, K_2\) vary strongly with \(T\)
  • Magnetite's \(K_1\) crosses zero at ~130 K — the isotropic point \(T_i\)
  • At \(T_i\) with the main \(K_1\) magnetocrystalline barriers gone, only the weak \(K_2\) term remains and spins wander far more freely — though shape and stress anisotropy remain
  • Below \(T_i\) the topology inverts: cube edges become easy, body diagonals hard

The same landscape at the isotropic point (130 K)

  • Same grain, same colorbar scale as the 300 K figure
  • \(K_1 = 0\): the [111] dimples and [100] bulges are gone — only the weak \(K_2\) term remains
  • The magnetocrystalline barriers have all but collapsed — shape, stress, and defect anisotropies persist

Rhetorical question lands on the next slide: low-temperature demagnetization. Cycling through the isotropic point + Verwey transition strips remanence held by walls pinned via magnetocrystalline anisotropy, while SD grains held by SHAPE anisotropy sail through — shape doesn't care about K1.

Low-temperature transitions & demagnetization

  • Cooling a room-temperature saturation remanence (RTSIRM) applied to a magnetite-bearing sample can lead to demagnetization due to two factors: • \(T_i \approx 130\ \mathrm{K}\): \(K_1 \rightarrow 0\), unpinning multidomain (MD) walls.
    • \(T_v \approx 120\ \mathrm{K}\): The cubic to monoclinic Verwey transition.
  • On warming, magnetization only partly recovers. The unpinned MD walls reorganize irreversibly, while stable single-domain (SD) magnetization largely survives.
This specimen is from our own Minnesota work — a magnetite-bearing basaltic dike from the East-Central Minnesota batholith study (Swanson-Hysell et al., 2021, Tectonics) — the same rocks as the course notebook examples. Groups will see exactly this experiment on the MPMS. Make sure to point out the sequential cooling steps: first we cross the isotropic point Ti (~130 K) where the magnetocrystalline barriers they just saw collapse, unpinning the domain walls. Then we cross the structural transition Tv (~120 K). The memory fraction is diagnostic: high memory = SD-dominated (or strongly pinned); big irreversible loss = MD magnetite. This irreversible loss is the physical basis of LTD (Low-Temperature Demagnetization)—a technique to clean soft MD overprints. Also flag: oxidized (maghemitized) magnetite suppresses/smears the Verwey transition — Thursday's low-T lecture goes deeper. If a condensed-matter-minded student pushes on the mechanism: the simple charge-freezing picture on the slide is the historical first approximation — the modern view is a coupled structural-electronic transition with charge/orbital ordering organized into three-site "trimeron" units (Senn et al., 2012, Nature).

Uniaxial anisotropy: hematite's easy plane

\(\epsilon_a = K_u\sin^2\theta\)
  • Sign of \(K_u\) defines the geometry: • \(K_u > 0\): Single easy axis
    • \(K_u < 0\): Perpendicular easy plane
  • Hematite's extreme contrast: • Out-of-plane is very hard: Perpendicular to the basal plane, \(K_u \approx -10^6\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\). The canted moment is locked in the plane.
    • In-plane is very easy: Basal plane anisotropy is \(\sim 5\) orders of magnitude weaker (\(\sim 10\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\)), pinned only by minor stress or defects.

Interactive from Tauxe & Swanson-Hysell (2026),
Essentials of Paleomagnetism ch. 4 (CC BY 4.0)

Warn the students about how to read this 3D shape: radius represents energy, not magnetization. So the giant yellow bulges at the poles are the hardest, high-energy directions (c-axis). The narrow dark-blue "waist" at the equator is the easy plane. Point out the physics of the 2D world: because the out-of-plane barrier is so massive (Ku is negative and ~10^6 J/m^3), it is incredibly hard to pull the canted moment out of the basal plane. But once you are inside that easy plane, the magnetocrystalline energy landscape is almost completely flat (only ~10 J/m^3). This means the spins can rotate incredibly freely in 2D, and are ultimately steered and pinned by tiny local stresses, defect fields, or shape effects.

Magnetoelastic anisotropy: stress & magnetic energy

\(\epsilon_\sigma = \tfrac{3}{2}\,\bar{\lambda}_s\,\sigma \sin^2\theta\)
  • Magnetoelastic Effect: Physical stress (\(\sigma\), Pa) deforms the lattice, shifting magnetic energy barriers.
  • Uniaxial Form: Stress acts as a virtual symmetry axis. The sign of \(\bar{\lambda}_s\sigma\) dictates whether it creates an easy axis or an easy plane.
  • The Scale (\(\bar{\lambda}_s \approx 35\text{--}40\times10^{-6}\)): • Maximum fractional length change is \(\sim 0.0038\%\).
    • For a \(1\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) grain, domain changes shift boundaries by only \(\sim 0.04\ \mathrm{nm}\) (less than typical interatomic spacing).

Why it matters in typical grains

  • Local Stress Fields: Even in unstressed rocks, crystal defects and dislocation networks produce complex, spatially varying internal stress fields (\(\sigma\)).
  • Energy Landscapes: These varying stress fields map directly onto a localized magnetic potential energy landscape.
  • Microscopic Pinning: Domain walls (MD) and vortex cores can get trapped in stress-induced energy wells, giving grains coercivity (\(H_c\)).
Distinguish "magnetostriction" (magnetization causing strain) from "magnetoelastic anisotropy" (stress changing magnetic energy). Stress (sigma) in the equation is in Pascals, not strain. The simple uniaxial equation relies on polycrystalline averaging and an isotropic lambda-bar. For a 1-micron grain, a domain shift moves boundaries by just 0.04 nm—smaller than typical interatomic spacing. In a typical grain, dislocation networks generate spatially varying stress fields. These map onto a complex, localized magnetic energy landscape. Domain walls and vortex cores get trapped in these energy wells, which is the exact microscopic mechanism that pins magnetization and generates coercivity (Hc).

Shape anisotropy

\(\mathbf{H}_d = -\mathbf{N}\cdot\mathbf{M}\)
  • The Demagnetizing Field (\(\mathbf{H}_d\)): Surface poles generate an internal magnetic field that directly opposes the grain's own magnetization (\(\mathbf{M}\)).
  • The Shape Factor (\(\mathbf{N}\)): A geometric tensor describing pole spacing. • Sphere: \(N = \tfrac{1}{3}\) in all directions. No geometric preference.
    • Prolate Ellipsoid: Poles are physically farther apart along the long axis (\(N_a < \tfrac{1}{3} < N_b\)).
  • The Easy Axis: Magnetizing along the long axis (\(\theta = 0^\circ\) in panel f) minimizes pole density, making it favored low-energy configuration.
Start by explaining that when a body is magnetized, free magnetic poles form on its outer boundaries. These "surface poles" act like a miniature magnet pointing backward, creating an internal "demagnetizing field" (Hd) that fights the grain's magnetization. Use the diagram to show this: in panel (a), the arrows show uniform magnetization to the right. This leaves north poles on the right and south poles on the left (panel b). These poles create an internal field Hd pointing to the left (panel c). Now explain how shape changes this. For a sphere (panel d), the poles are symmetrically distributed, so N is 1/3 in all directions. Changing the angle theta of magnetization (panel d) has no energy penalty. But if we stretch the sphere into a prolate ellipsoid (cigar shape, panel e), magnetizing along the long 'a' axis keeps the poles far apart, reducing Hd. Point out panel (f): theta is the angle between magnetization (M) and this long axis (a). When theta = 0, the poles are furthest apart, making the long axis "magnetostatically favored" (the easy axis). As theta rotates toward 90 degrees, Hd spikes, creating a high energy barrier.

Shape-anisotropy energy scales as \(M_s^2\)

\(K_{\mathrm{shape}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta N\,\mu_0 M_s^2\)

Magnetite (\(M_s \approx 480\ \mathrm{kA/m}\))

Shape can be a dominant anisotropy

  • For an ideal prolate spheroid with moderate elongation (\(a/b = 1.5\)):
    \(\Delta N \approx 0.151 \rightarrow K_{\mathrm{shape}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\)
  • This is comparable to or larger than magnetite's room-temperature magnetocrystalline anisotropy scale: \(|K_1| \approx 1.1\text{--}1.3 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\).
  • Takeaway: Shape strongly influences the easy axis and magnetic stability of elongated, near-single-domain magnetite.

Hematite (\(M_s \approx 2\ \mathrm{kA/m}\))

Negligible shape anisotropy

  • Because energy scales with the square of saturation magnetization (\(M_s^2\)):
    \(M_s^2\) is \(\sim 5\times10^4\) smaller!
  • The same moderate geometry yields a negligible shape energy density: \(K_{\mathrm{shape}} \approx 0.4\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\).
  • Takeaway: Hematite coercivity is generally governed by intrinsic and magnetoelastic anisotropy, not by magnetostatic shape anisotropy.
This slide illustrates how the square-law dependence of shape anisotropy on saturation magnetization (Ms^2) leads to completely different physical behaviors in magnetite versus hematite. On the left, look at magnetite. It has a high Ms. If we moderately stretch an ideal magnetite grain to an aspect ratio of 1.5, the shape anisotropy energy (K_shape) is roughly 2.2 x 10^4 J/m^3. This meets or exceeds the scale of magnetite's bulk magnetocrystalline anisotropy constant (K1 is negative, with an absolute value of 1.1 to 1.3 x 10^4 J/m^3). Be careful to point out to the students that crystal anisotropy still matters—especially for realistic faceted crystals, surface effect variations, or microstructures—but shape behaves as a dominant, highly influential force in determining the easy axis and switching barriers of near-single-domain magnetite. This is why stable carriers like magnetofossils or plagioclase exsolution needles rely heavily on shape. Now, look at hematite on the right. Because it's an antiferromagnet with a weak canted moment, its Ms is tiny (about 2 kA/m). Because shape energy scales with Ms^2, its K_shape drops by nearly 50,000 times compared to magnetite. In the exact same geometry, its shape anisotropy is a mere 0.4 J/m^3. Explain to the students that this means the magnetostatic shape of hematite's weak net moment is negligible. Instead, hematite's high coercive stability is mostly governed by intrinsic and magnetoelastic anisotropy (associated with strain, defects, and dislocations).

Part 4

The flipping field:
Stoner–Wohlfarth

Microscopic coercivity: the field that flips a moment

Anisotropy sets the energy barrier; the barrier sets the field needed to force a uniformly magnetized (single domain) particle over it:

Magnetocrystalline (Cubic): \(\mu_0 H_k = \dfrac{4}{3}\dfrac{|K_1|}{M_s}\)
Shape: \(\mu_0 H_k = \mu_0\,\Delta N\, M_s\)
Magnetite grainDominant anisotropyμ₀Hk
equantmagnetocrystalline (K₁)≈ 37 mT
a/b = 1.5shape≈ 90 mT
a/b = 2shape≈ 145 mT
needle (a/b → ∞)shape≈ 300 mT

Also called the flipping field, switching field, or intrinsic coercivity. These are single-grain, coherent-rotation values: the bulk coercivity of a random assemblage is about half of \(H_k\), and interactions, vortex states, and domain walls pull measured \(H_c\) lower still.

Preview these numbers to calibrate expectations for their VSM lab data. Magnetite coercivities generally live between ~10 and 300 mT depending on shape and grain size. In contrast, hematite can easily reach multiple tesla. This is NOT because of weak magnetocrystalline anisotropy in its basal plane, but because its high coercivity is dominated by magnetoelastic (strain) anisotropy and defect pinning. When you divide even a modest strain-energy barrier by hematite's tiny Ms, the switching field spikes into the tesla range. Note the unit hygiene point from the chapter: coercivity derived theoretically is an H-field (A/m), but laboratory instruments report μ₀H in tesla. *Pro-tip for Q&A:* In engineering, "intrinsic coercivity" (Hci) refers specifically to the bulk M-H loop crossing point (where M=0) compared to normal B-H loop coercivity. In rock magnetism, we almost exclusively measure M-H loops, so we often use "coercivity" and "intrinsic coercivity" interchangeably, using "microscopic coercivity" (Hk) specifically for the single-domain particle switching limit.

Let a student drive this one. The moment tracks its local minimum as the field grows; watch the second derivative panel — when the curvature at the occupied minimum hits zero, the well ceases to exist and the moment rolls into the global minimum. Irreversible: back the field off and it stays flipped. This single mechanism, summed over a population, IS the hysteresis loop.

The flipping field depends on the field's angle

\(\mu_0 H_f = \dfrac{2K_u}{M_s}\, \dfrac{1}{(\cos^{2/3}\phi + \sin^{2/3}\phi)^{3/2}}\)
  • Maximum at \(\phi = 0°\): the microscopic coercivity \(\mu_0H_k = 2K_u/M_s\) from Part 3
  • Minimum at 45° — grains at intermediate angles flip easiest (at \(\mu_0H_k/2\))
  • At exactly \(\phi = 90°\): no irreversible switching at any field — the moment rotates reversibly and springs back
Three anchors to call out: 0° gives the full 2Ku/Ms; 45° gives half that; 90° gives reversible rotation with no remanence change. A randomly oriented rock contains grains at ALL these angles simultaneously — so a natural specimen's response is this whole curve averaged, which is exactly what the widgets in the next part compute.

A uniaxial particle in an applied field

A prolate magnetite grain with elongation a/b = 2, field applied at φ = 45° to the easy axis. Anisotropy energy \(\epsilon_a = K_u \sin^2\theta\) digs two minima (the easy axis); the field adds the interaction energy \(\epsilon_m = -M_sB\cos(\phi-\theta)\), which tilts the landscape. A 30 mT field tilts it; 500 mT dominates it.

\(\epsilon_t = K_u \sin^2\theta \;-\; M_s B \cos(\phi - \theta)\)

Tauxe & Swanson-Hysell (2026), Essentials of Paleomagnetism (CC BY 4.0).

Set up the cast: theta = moment angle from the easy axis; phi = field angle from the easy axis. Anisotropy digs two wells (0° and 180°); the field tilts the landscape toward phi. The moment sits in whichever LOCAL minimum it occupies — not necessarily the global one. That's the entire origin of hysteresis: history decides which well you're in.

Part 5

Hysteresis loops

The experiment: a vibrating sample magnetometer

  • Specimen on a rod between the pole pieces of an electromagnet (applied field \(B\), up to ~1.8 T on our Lake Shore VSM)
  • Specimen vibrates at a known frequency → its moment produces an oscillating flux in pickup coils → induced voltage ∝ magnetic moment
  • A lock-in amplifier extracts the signal at the drive frequency → sensitive measurement of \(m\) as a function of \(B\)
  • Protocol: ramp to a (hoped-for) saturating field, sweep down through zero to the opposite extreme, and back: the hysteresis loop

One group ran such experiments yesterday afternoon on D-6A specimens — every group will this week.

  • Loop shape encodes: mineralogy, domain state, grain size, SP fractions, and the dia/paramagnetic matrix

Explore: one grain's loop, angle by angle

Exact Stoner–Wohlfarth physics: the moment rides the occupied minimum of \(\epsilon_t = K_u\sin^2\theta - M_sB\cos(\phi-\theta)\), flipping only when that minimum vanishes (at the marked \(\mu_0H_f\)). At φ = 0°: a square loop closing at ±μ₀Hf. At φ = 45°: flipping at half the field, with reversible rotation rounding the corners. At φ = 90°: no flip — a closed, fully reversible curve. Between 76.7° and 90°: the little cusp at ±μ₀Hf is real — the flip reduces the projection along B, so m∥ overshoots just before switching (Stoner & Wohlfarth, 1948).

Reproduce the chapter's loop-gallery live: 0° square; 22° and 70° share nearly the same flipping field (the H_f(φ) curve is symmetric about 45°) but very different loop heights; 90° collapses to a reversible line. The projection matters: what the VSM measures is the moment component along B, which is why even the square loop's plateau sits below 1 for oblique angles. Slide the angle to ~85° for a surprise: above 76.7° the flip actually decreases the component along B (the moment swings past the field direction), so the branch overshoots into a small cusp at H_f — a genuine SW-1948 feature, not a glitch. Next slide: add up a whole population of these.

A rock: millions of randomly oriented grains

  • Stoner–Wohlfarth Ensemble: The bulk loop is the integrated sum of independent, random 3-D easy-axis orientations.
  • Squareness (\(M_r / M_s\)): Removing the field lets moments relax to their nearest easy axis. For any uniaxial shape, this geometry yields:
    \(M_r = 0.5\,M_s\) exactly
  • Bulk Coercivity (\(\mu_0 H_c\)): While the flipping field for these individual grains is 100 mT, a randomly oriented assemblage has a collective coercivity of:
    48 mT
Emphasize that the 0.5 squareness value is pure 3D spatial geometry. When you saturate a random ensemble and release the field, each individual single-domain moment falls back to its NEAREST easy axis hemisphere. The mathematical average of cos(psi) over a hemisphere is exactly 0.5. This 0.5 value serves as a major baseline calibration mark in rock magnetism. If students measure a ratio close to 0.5 in the lab, they are looking at textbook uniaxial single-domain behavior. If they measure a very low ratio like 0.1 or lower, they are dealing with multi-domain or vortex states, which is highly likely for their real-world rock samples. For the coercivity line, highlight how orientation softens the system: even though an individual grain requires a 100 mT flipping field when perfectly aligned, hitting a random mix of them from all angles drops the collective threshold of the rock down to just 48 mT. You can point right to the blue Bc dot on the widget to show them exactly where that 48 mT manifests on the plot.

Walking the loop, state by state

Use this as the vocabulary slide. Walk the four numbered states and have the students describe what the moment population looks like at each point: 1. State 1 is demagnetized. The moments are completely balanced on their easy axes, meaning the net M is 0. Point out the initial slope—that first response is the low-field susceptibility (chi_lf). 2. State 2 is saturation. The external field is strong enough to pull all moments parallel to it, maximizing M at Ms. 3. State 3 is remanence. Turn the field off, and the moments relax back to their nearest easy direction. For uniaxial SD grains, this geometry means Mr is exactly half of Ms. 4. State 4 is coercivity. Applying a reverse field flips exactly half of the net moment, bringing the bulk magnetization back to zero. *Segue to next slide:* "If we don't go all the way to saturation in State 2, but stop partway and turn the field off, we acquire what is called an Isothermal Remanent Magnetization (IRM). If we do go all the way to saturation first, we call that a saturation IRM (sIRM). Let's look at how we measure that..."

Real loops: parameters after slope correction

  • \(\chi_{lf}\) — initial slope from a demagnetized state
  • \(\chi_{hf}\) — slope after ferromagnetic saturation: primarily para + dia → subtract it (unsaturated hematite/goethite adds curvature — see approach to saturation, Part 7)
  • \(M_s\) — saturation magnetization (after correction)
  • \(M_r\) — saturation remanence (sIRM)
  • \(\mu_0H_c\) — coercivity: in-field \(M = 0\)
  • \(\mu_0H_{cr}\) — coercivity of remanence: the backfield that zeros the remanence (next slides)

Your D-6A troctolites: olivine + plagioclase matrix → expect a strong paramagnetic \(\chi_{hf}\) on top of the magnetite loop.

  • process_hyst_loop implements the IRM decision tree (Jackson & Solheid, 2010): gridding, centering & drift checks, quality factor \(Q\), saturation tests, then the appropriate high-field correction
  • The hysteresis_processing_walk_through notebook unpacks every step — the next three slides preview the key ones

A loop is a mixture of the ferromagnetic and dia/paramagnetic responses

A synthetic "troctolite": single-domain magnetite (Stoner–Wohlfarth loop, Ms = 92 Am²/kg for pure magnetite) in a paramagnetic olivine-rich matrix. The high-field slope (fit at |B| > 0.7 Bmax) is the paramagnetic susceptibility; subtracting it closes the loop and yields Ms, Mr, and Mr/Ms. Try a low magnetite content: the raw loop looks almost like a paramagnet — the correction rescues the ferromagnetic signal.

This is exactly what process_hyst_loops does to their data. Set magnetite to ~0.03 wt% and the raw "loop" is nearly a straight paramagnetic line — which is what some of the troctolite specimens may look like. Apply the correction: a clean SD loop pops out with Mr/Ms = 0.5 because this synthetic is pure SD. Their REAL specimens won't give 0.5 — that discrepancy is the next slide and the setup for Max.

When the loop doesn't saturate: approach to saturation

  • The linear \(\chi_{hf}\) correction assumes the ferromagnetic loop is saturated in the fitting window — if it isn't, unresolved curvature is absorbed into the slope and \(M_s\) is underestimated (and \(M_r/M_s\) overestimated)
  • process_hyst_loop F-tests high-field linearity (windows above 60/70/80% of peak field); when all fail, it fits the approach-to-saturation model \(M(H) = \chi_{hf}H + M_s + a_1H^{-1} + a_2H^{-2}\) instead (fit_type='Fabian' for the Fabian 2006 power law)

If the statistics say "not saturated" even for the nonlinear fit, \(M_s\) is a minimum estimate at your peak field — watch for this in the felsic-group and any hematite/goethite-bearing specimens.

The practical trap: an unsaturated loop LOOKS fine after a linear correction — panel (b) shows the artificially flattened orange curve you'd happily accept. The bias hides in Ms (and therefore in Mr/Ms, and therefore in the Day-plot position). Classic case: the remagnetized Appalachian carbonates with Mr/Ms > 0.5 interpreted as cubic anisotropy — reprocessing with approach-to-saturation fits (Jackson & Solheid, 2012) raised Ms and brought Mr/Ms back below 0.5. The 60/70/80% windows plus the F statistic catch it objectively; here F_NL = 98.7, 25.2, 17.5. High-coercivity minerals (hematite, goethite) are Thursday's story — but the felsic group may meet them first.

Inside the processing: split the loop into two curves

\(M_{rh} = \tfrac{1}{2}(M^+ - M^-)\)
\(M_{ih} = \tfrac{1}{2}(M^+ + M^-)\)
  • \(M_{rh}\) — everything irreversible (switching): \(M_r = M_{rh}(0)\); its median field \(B_{rh}\) is a loop-derived proxy for \(B_{cr}\)
  • \(M_{ih}\) — everything reversible (rotation + para/dia)
  • \(M_{rh}\) should be even, \(M_{ih}\) odd — deviations feed the loop-closure test and quality factor \(Q\)

Jackson & Solheid (2010).

The decomposition is the workhorse inside process_hyst_loop: Mrh isolates the switching (its half-width Brh is a loop-derived stand-in for Bcr — no backfield experiment needed), and the even/odd symmetry requirements give quantitative quality control: the mismatch between the upper branch and the inverted lower branch defines the noise for the Q statistic, and the high-field tail of Mrh tests loop closure. When students see Q, Qf, and the closure flag in their results table, this is what's behind them.

Part 6

Beyond uniform:
flower, vortex — and the road to domains

The problem with staying uniform

  • A uniformly magnetized (single domain, SD) configuration incurs the maximum possible magnetostatic self-energy: \(E_d = \epsilon_d\,v \propto M_s^2\,v\) — a total that scales with the cube of grain diameter
  • Exchange energy wants spins strictly parallel; self-energy wants the external magnetic field (the surface poles) eliminated
  • Exchange wins the budget fight only in the smallest grains (≲ 60–70 nm for equant magnetite)
  • Larger grains minimize this energy penalty by letting the magnetization curl: spins rotate gradually (low exchange cost per neighbor) as the external field — and the self-energy — collapse

Micromagnetic models (e.g., MERRILL) find the configurations that minimize the total: watch the states evolve →

Frame this as a competing energy budget: The magnetostatic self-energy (or stray-field energy) is at its absolute maximum when magnetization is perfectly uniform, and because it is a volume-based integration, it scales with the cube of the grain's diameter (d³). Any non-uniform state—like curling or domain walls—introduces a domestic "cost" in exchange energy, but because those transitions occur across surfaces or boundary planes, that cost scales roughly with the cross-sectional area (d²). Below the crossover diameter, exchange wins and the grain stays uniform. Above it, the d³ scaling of the self-energy becomes too massive, forcing the system into a structured, flux-closed state to minimize total energy. Point out that while the classical 20th-century cartoons jump straight from uniform SD to discrete, striped domain walls (as shown in the figure), modern micromagnetics (which we'll see on the next slide) reveal a beautiful intermediate phase of continuous curling and vortices first.

What micromagnetics reveals: SD → flower → vortex

Equant: uniform at 80 nm → flower (surface spins splay) at 90 nm → vortex by 140 nm. Elongate: SD survives to larger sizes, and the vortex core aligns with the long axis.

Walk through the picture slowly — this is the modern replacement for the SD/PSD/MD cartoon. Flower: the self energy first wins only at the CORNERS, where surface poles are concentrated, so edge spins fan out while the interior stays coherent. Vortex: past ~80-100 nm the whole magnetization rolls up; neighboring spins are still nearly parallel (exchange is locally happy) but the flux now closes internally. Elongate grains resist longer because shape anisotropy stabilizes the uniform state — another reason elongate SD magnetite is the paleomagnetic workhorse.

Explore: the energy budget behind the domain states

Drag the slider to see how the uniform state's self-energy (\(\propto d^3\)) overtakes the area-dependent energy cost (\(\propto d^2\)) of a curled, flux-closed configuration, leading to the departure from single-domain behavior.

Spin structures are schematic 2D slices. Threshold boundaries for equant magnetite modeled after Nagy et al. (2017) and Essentials of Paleomagnetism (Ch. 4). Absolute energies are highly geometry-dependent; the scaling argument is the key takeaway.

Start at 40 nm: uniform, and the red d³ curve is far below the green d² curve — no incentive to curl. Slide to ~75 nm: flower — the curves are closing. Past ~85 nm the vortex takes over: point out the core symbols (out-of-plane) — that thin core is what carries the grain's remanence, and the circulating spins cancel. Past 200 nm: walls and true domains — and that's where I hand off to Max at 11. The net-remanence readout makes the efficiency collapse visceral: from 100% (SD) to a few % (MD).

The vortex state can be a faithful recorder

  • The net moment of a vortex grain is carried almost entirely by the narrow core; the circulating spins cancel
  • Vortex states can be remarkably stable: computed relaxation times exceed the age of the Solar System (Nagy et al., 2017)
  • Behavior is SD-like in many experiments → historically labeled "pseudo-single domain" (PSD)
  • Modern usage: call the states what they are — flower, single vortex, multi-vortex — "PSD" survives as the label for the in-between hysteresis behavior

Why it matters for D-6A: many of the magnetite grains in intrusive rocks like the Duluth Complex will be above true SD size — vortex-state (and MD) grains likely dominate what you are measuring this week.

\(\delta_w = \pi\sqrt{A/K} \approx 90\ \mathrm{nm}\)
\(\epsilon_w = 2\pi\sqrt{AK} \approx 3\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}\)
The wall math delivers a beautiful consistency check: the equilibrium wall width in magnetite (~90 nm) is BIGGER than the grains we just called vortex-state — so "the smallest wall is really more like the vortex state," and true walls only fit in grains of a few hundred nm. Theory and micromagnetics agree. Hand-off framing: Max picks up there — wall pinning, Barkhausen jumps, and what MD hysteresis looks like.

The backfield experiment: isolating the remanence

Contrast with the in-field loop: here every measurement is made in ZERO field, so reversible rotations contribute nothing — the backfield curve is pure irreversible switching. That's why its derivative is the cleanest routinely-measured coercivity spectrum, and why coercivity unmixing (Thursday, magnetic mineralogy) is done on curves like this. The two-component example in the figure previews exactly that.

Explore: a simulated backfield experiment

Same Stoner–Wohlfarth population as before (3-D random easy axes; each grain flips at \(B_K\cdot h_f(\psi)\)). Run the experiment: grains flip in order of switching field (left panel) and the remanence walks from +SIRM through zero at \(B_{cr}\). For ideal SD grains \(H_{cr}/H_c \approx 1.09\) — nearly equal. Multidomain grains push the ratio far higher (≈ 5) — one axis of the Day plot, coming next.

Two things to milk: (1) with σ = 0 the population still has a coercivity spectrum — the spread comes purely from easy-axis ORIENTATION via h_f(ψ), peaking near B_K/2 (the 45° grains flip first); (2) the in-field curve (grey) crosses zero before the remanence curve does — H_c < H_cr always, because in-field reversible rotation helps pull M through zero. For SD the two are nearly equal (ratio ~1.09); wall motion in MD grains decouples them (~5). Crank σ up to see a broadened natural-looking spectrum.

Why Bcr = 0.525 BK — and why the IRM logo looks like that

sIRM state (Mr = 0.5 Ms) After saturating along +B, every moment sits on the easy-axis direction in the +B hemisphere: the occupied directions fill a half-space, and the average of \(\cos\psi\) is exactly ½.
DC demagnetized state (Mr = 0) At −0.525 BK exactly the axes 30–60° from B flip — \(h_f(\psi)\) is lowest there — and their moments swing through the origin to the −B side. Black = where moments now point: the flipped bands cancel the survivors. Still half dark, but M = 0.
Look familiar? The Institute for Rock Magnetism's logo is exactly this pair of Stoner–Wohlfarth diagrams — the sIRM half-disk and the DC-demagnetized pinwheel, every wedge boundary on the 30° grid.

This is why \(B_{cr} = 0.525\,B_K\) for an ideal uniaxial SD assemblage (\(h_f = 0.525\) at ψ = 30° and 60°) while \(B_c = 0.48\,B_K\) — the ratio \(H_{cr}/H_c \approx 1.09\) you just saw emerge from the simulation.

The payoff slide for the backfield physics — and a piece of IRM lore. Walk it: (1) at sIRM the occupied easy-axis directions fill the +B hemisphere; (2) a backfield eats orientation space from the 45° band outward, because h_f(ψ) has its minimum there; (3) at the field where the flipped band's moment cancels the survivors' — h = 0.525 for the ideal case — the remanence passes through zero, and the occupied directions make the pinwheel. Bruce Moskowitz taught this with these exact diagrams for years, and the Institute put them in its logo.
